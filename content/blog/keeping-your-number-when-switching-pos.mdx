The number printed on your pizza boxes may not belong to you. Not in the sense that someone could steal it, but in the plainer sense that a company you pay every month is the registered owner and can decline to hand it over when you leave.

This surprises operators constantly, because nothing about the arrangement feels like a transfer of ownership. You signed up for a point-of-sale system with online ordering. During onboarding, someone said "we'll set up a number for your online orders" or "we'll handle the phone side." A number appeared. It works. It has worked for three years. It is on your menus.

It is on the vendor's account.

## How your number ends up in someone else's name

There are three common paths, and none of them involve bad faith.

The first is provisioning. A vendor needs a phone number to make their product work, so they buy one from a wholesale carrier and point it at your business. The line item on your invoice, if there is one at all, says something like "communications" or nothing. Whoever bought the number is the customer of record with the carrier. That is the vendor.

The second is call tracking. A marketing agency or a POS company with an analytics dashboard wants to know how many calls their product generated, so they issue a tracking number that forwards to your real line. Useful. The problem starts when someone updates your Google Business Profile, your Yelp page, and your website footer to display the tracking number, because that is the only way the tracking works. Now the number that customers actually dial is the vendor's, and your real number has quietly become the one nobody uses.

The third is a port that went the wrong direction. You had a real number. During onboarding, a vendor offered to "take care of the phone" and you signed a letter of authorization without reading which account the number was moving into. It moved into theirs.

### The version that hurts most

The catering line. Restaurants often add a second number for catering or large orders, precisely because that traffic is worth more per call. That second number is almost always the one a vendor provisioned, since it did not exist before the vendor showed up. It is also the number attached to your highest-value customers, sitting in your event contracts and your corporate clients' address books.

If you have a catering line you did not personally order from a carrier, check that one first.

## How to find out, in about twenty minutes

Follow the money. A phone number you own generates a bill from a telecommunications carrier addressed to your business, with the number listed on it. Pull your last twelve months of invoices and find that line. If you cannot find a carrier billing you for that specific number, you probably do not own it.

Second, look at your onboarding paperwork with the vendor. Search it for the words "number," "porting," "telephone," and "authorization." Most agreements say something, and what they say is usually clearer than what you were told verbally.

Third, ask directly, in writing, and ask a specific question rather than a general one. Not "do we own our number." Ask: "Is our business the customer of record on the account holding this number, and if we gave notice tomorrow, what is your process and timeline for releasing it?" Vague answers are answers. A vendor who owns your number and intends to hold it over you will not put a release timeline in writing.

Fourth, check what number is publicly displayed. Look at your own Google listing, Yelp, your website, your delivery app pages, and your printed materials. Write down every number you find. If they are not all the same, the differences are tracking numbers, and each one is a dependency on whoever issued it. Getting all of that consistent is its own project, covered in [keeping your listings consistent](/blog/nap-consistency-restaurants).

## What it costs you when it breaks

The failure is not dramatic. Nothing goes dark. What happens is that you switch systems, the old number stops forwarding, and calls to the number on every menu you have printed since 2023 hit a message telling the caller the number is not in service. Some of them try again. Most of them order somewhere else, and you never learn it happened.

Then there is the listing damage. Your Google Business Profile with the disconnected number keeps ranking for a while, sending callers into a dead end. Directory sites that scraped that number years ago will keep publishing it long after you have corrected the source. Old phone numbers are remarkably persistent on the internet.

And there is the quiet one: your regulars have you saved in their contacts under the old number. Nothing you do to your listings reaches their phones.

## Fixing it before you need to

If you own the number outright, the work is administrative and mostly about not losing it during a future transition. The sequence for doing that safely is in [the phone number porting guide](/blog/phone-number-porting-guide-restaurants), and the short version is that you never cancel the old service before the port completes.

If a vendor owns it and you are staying with them for now, your goal is to get the number into your name while everyone is still friendly. Ask for it as a contract amendment at renewal, while you still have something they want and no announced intent to leave. Some vendors will do this readily because they never wanted to be your phone company. Others will not, and their refusal is information about the relationship.

If a vendor owns it and you are already leaving, act early and in parallel. Send the release request the same week you give notice, not after the migration is scheduled. Simultaneously, start publishing a number you do control, and set up forwarding from the old one for as long as the vendor will keep it alive. A number you own and forward is better than a number you rent and lose.

The general principle applies to any vendor holding an asset your business runs on, which is the same argument made about your call recordings and order history in [data portability when you leave](/blog/data-portability-when-you-leave) and about the clauses in [voice AI contract terms to avoid](/blog/voice-ai-contract-terms-to-avoid).

## The clause to ask for at signing

One sentence, in every agreement with any vendor that touches your phone: any telephone number used in connection with your business is registered to your business, and will be released or ported at your written request within a stated number of days.

Ask for it before you sign anything, including with us. Vendors who intend to keep your number will negotiate on that sentence, and the negotiation tells you everything. Vendors who never wanted your number will agree in a sentence, and the whole issue disappears for the life of the relationship. The same applies when you evaluate replacing an existing system, which is why [switching vendors](/blog/switching-voice-ai-vendors) starts with an inventory rather than a demo.

Go pull one invoice this afternoon and find the carrier that bills you for your main line. If that invoice exists, you are fine and you can stop thinking about this. If it does not, you have found a dependency you did not know you had, and it is much cheaper to resolve today than during a migration.
