Most owners think call forwarding is one setting. On an AT&T business line it is usually three, and the two people skip are the ones that decide what happens during your dinner rush.

The confusion is understandable. `*72` is the code everyone knows, it does something visible, and it works. It's also the blunt instrument, and if it's the only tool you use you'll either send every call away when you didn't mean to or send none of the ones that matter.

## Three settings, not one

### Forward all calls

Dial `*72` from the line, wait for the stutter tone, dial the ten-digit destination, and wait for it to answer. `*73` turns it off. Once this is on, the handset in your restaurant does not ring. Not quietly, not once. The call never arrives.

This is the correct setting for a restaurant running a voice agent full time, because there's no reason for a phone to ring on your line at all if the agent takes the order and transfers the calls that need a human. It is the wrong setting the first week, when you still want to hear what's happening.

### Forward when busy

Fires only when the line is already in use. On AT&T this is a separately subscribed feature, commonly activated with `*90` and cancelled with `*91`, though the codes vary by region and by how old the line is. Confirm yours with business support instead of trusting a code you found in a forum.

For a single-line restaurant this is the most underrated setting in the building. Every second caller during a rush currently hears a busy signal or rolls to voicemail, and neither of those is a customer.

### Forward when there's no answer

Fires after a set number of rings, commonly `*92` to activate and `*93` to cancel, again subject to what's provisioned. The ring count is usually configurable through support and defaults higher than you want. Ask for three rings, not six.

The trap here is the interaction with voicemail. AT&T voicemail is itself a no-answer forward. If both are set, whichever fires first wins, and it's usually not the one you configured last. If you're forwarding on no-answer to a voice agent, the voicemail box has to be taken out of the path or it will keep catching calls and you'll spend a week blaming the agent.

## Remote access, and why it's worth the line item

Star codes have a physical requirement that nobody mentions until it bites: you have to be standing at the phone.

Remote Access to Call Forwarding removes that. AT&T gives you an access number and a PIN; you call in from anywhere, identify the line, and use the same codes. It's a small monthly charge and it pays for itself the first time you're closed for a burst pipe, a funeral, or a health inspection and need every caller to hear something other than a ring.

Ask for it when you set up forwarding, not after. Adding it later is another call to support and another wait.

## AT&T sells several different phone products

The instructions above cover traditional AT&T business lines. If your restaurant is on AT&T Phone for Business over fiber, or on AT&T Office@Hand, the star codes may work but the web portal is what actually governs the line, and the two can hold conflicting settings.

The rule is the same as anywhere else: configure in one place. If there's a portal, use the portal, and treat the handset codes as an emergency override you use once and then reconcile. Fighting a portal with star codes produces a phone system where nobody can explain what happens to an incoming call, which is a genuinely expensive state to be in during a Friday service.

## Where these setups actually fail

The failure is almost never the star code. It's what sits between the code and the customer.

Hunt groups are the big one. If your published number rolls to a second line when the first is busy, forwarding the first line does nothing for the roll. The caller lands on line two, which is still ringing an empty office. Every number in the group needs the same treatment, or the group's overflow destination itself has to change. This is the single most common reason an operator tells me forwarding "didn't do anything."

The second is the wrong number. The number on your menus, your Google listing, and your delivery apps is not always the number AT&T shows as your main line, especially if a marketing company issued a tracking number at some point. Forward the number customers actually dial, which you verify by calling it from a phone outside the building rather than by reading the bill.

The third is shared-line equipment. Card terminals, alarm panels, and kitchen fax machines that dial out over a voice line will break under forward-all, and they break silently. Check what's plugged in before you flip anything.

The fourth is nobody testing. Forwarding either works or it doesn't, and the check takes one call. Make it part of the opening routine for two weeks. Details of what to watch in that first fortnight are in the [onboarding checklist](/blog/ai-phone-onboarding-checklist-restaurants).

## Forwarding versus moving the number

Forwarding leaves the number on your AT&T account and passes calls along. Porting moves it outright. Start with forwarding, always. It's reversible with one dialed code, it requires no paperwork, and it lets you run a real dinner service on the new setup before committing anything. If it goes badly, you dial `*73` and you're back where you started in four seconds.

Porting is worth considering after a month of stable operation, and the process, along with what genuinely goes wrong during it, is in [porting a restaurant phone number](/blog/phone-number-porting-guide-restaurants). ## The four-minute test

Set the forward at 2pm on a quiet day. Then, from a cell phone that has never called your restaurant, place four calls in a row: a normal pickup order, a question about closing time, a request to speak to a manager, and one where you go silent and wait.

Read the POS and the call log after. If the pickup order landed correctly and the manager request reached an actual human, you have a working configuration. If the silent call ended up somewhere you didn't expect, that's the path an intoxicated caller, a bad connection, and half your elderly customers will also take, so fix it before Friday.
