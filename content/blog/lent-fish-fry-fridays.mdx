Ash Wednesday and six Fridays. Seven days, maybe eight if you count Good Friday separately, and for a lot of neighborhood seafood houses, pubs, and parish-adjacent kitchens those days carry a serious share of the first quarter.

The phone problem inside those days is unusually specific. One menu, one narrow service window, and a supply of fish that was ordered a week earlier and does not replenish when it runs out.

## The window is earlier and tighter than a normal Friday

Fish fry volume builds from around 4:30 and peaks before most kitchens think dinner has started. The crowd skews toward families and older customers who are eating before something else, and the tail after 7:30 is thin enough that you'll be standing around.

So your busiest phone hour is 4:30 to 5:30, which on a normal Friday is your quietest. The crew that will handle the rush is arriving during it. Whoever grabs the phone at 4:45 is doing it between other tasks.

The calls that ring out in that hour never show up anywhere. They aren't in your POS, they aren't in your reservation book, and the night still looks busy, so nothing prompts you to go look. Counting them takes about ten minutes with the method in [how many calls does your restaurant miss](/blog/how-many-calls-does-your-restaurant-miss), and Lent Fridays are a good week to do it because the miss rate is concentrated enough to be obvious.

## The 86 problem is worse here than anywhere

Most restaurants 86 an item occasionally. A fish fry runs the risk on a different scale, because you bought a fixed number of cases and the whole night's demand is pointed at one plate.

When the cod goes at 6:40, three things need to happen inside a minute: the kitchen stops promising it, the phone stops selling it, and the callers already holding an order for it get a real alternative rather than a surprise at the counter.

The middle one is the one that breaks. A person answering the phone finds out the fish is gone when the expediter yells it, and in the meantime they've sold nine more. Every one of those is a phone call back or an argument at pickup, at exactly the moment your line is deepest. Keeping availability synced between the kitchen and whatever answers your phone is the specific fix, and the failure modes are laid out in [real-time 86ing and menu sync](/blog/real-time-86ing-menu-sync).

### Decide your fallback before you need it

When the primary fish goes, the answer should already exist. Haddock if the cod is out, a shrimp plate, a baked option, whatever your kitchen can produce without a new prep. Write it down, price it, and tell the phone what it is.

A caller who's told "we're out of cod, but the haddock plate is ready in twenty" mostly says yes. A caller who's told "we're out of fish" hangs up and orders pizza.

Getting the case count right in the first place is a season-long exercise rather than a single decision. Order too little and you close the fryer at 6:45 on your biggest night of the quarter. Order too much and you're eating the difference, because fresh fish does not wait a week for the next Friday. Track what you actually sold each Friday, note whether you ran out and at what time, and adjust the next week's order against that rather than against a feeling. By the third Friday you'll have a number worth trusting.

## One menu is an advantage if you configure it that way

Lent Fridays are one of the rare services where the menu narrows instead of expanding. Two or three plates, a few sides, a kids option. That simplicity is worth exploiting.

A short menu means the phone conversation is short, which means throughput. It also means whatever takes your orders, human or otherwise, can be set up tightly for the season rather than handling your whole card. Loading a seasonal menu as a dated configuration and rolling it back after Easter is ordinary work, described in [seasonal menu updates for a voice agent](/blog/seasonal-menu-updates-voice-agent).

The questions callers ask during this season are also narrow and repetitive:

- Is the fish baked or fried, and do you have both.
- What comes with the plate, and can the side be swapped.
- How long is the wait right now.
- Do you have a drive-up or curbside option.

Those four account for a large share of the calls that aren't orders, and none of them require a person. Handling them without pulling someone off the line is the plain version of what [answering hours and parking FAQs](/blog/voice-ai-answering-faqs-hours-parking) covers.

## Quote a time you can hold, and stagger the pickups

Fryer capacity is a hard ceiling. You can take orders faster than you can produce fish, and during a fish fry that gap opens within twenty minutes of the rush starting.

The discipline is the same as any capacity-limited service: quote a specific clock time, not a vague duration, and let that time stretch as the queue deepens rather than repeating a number out of habit. [Quoting accurate pickup times](/blog/quoting-accurate-pickup-times) walks through how to set it against real production rates instead of optimism.

Pre-orders are the other half. If you can move a third of your volume into named slots between 4:00 and 5:30, you've converted a reactive rush into a schedule. Offer specific times rather than asking what works, because "we have 4:45 or 5:15" fills and "when would you like it" produces a pile of 6:30s.

## Know which parishes are running a fry, and stop competing on price

In a lot of neighborhoods, several churches run their own fish fry on the same Fridays. They serve a tight window, at a price you can't match, to a crowd that was going there regardless.

Trying to undercut that is a losing game. What works better is being the option for the people it doesn't serve: anyone eating after 7, anyone who wants pickup rather than a hall, anyone who wants something other than a fried cod plate, and anyone ordering for a household rather than sitting down. Find out which parishes near you run one and on which weeks, because the Fridays they're dark are your biggest nights and you should staff differently for them.

At the end of the season, pull one comparison. Answered calls between 4:30 and 6:00 on your fish fry Fridays, against the same window on your ordinary Fridays. If the answered share fell, the season didn't just make you busy, it found your phone's ceiling. That ceiling doesn't go away in April, it just stops being visible.
