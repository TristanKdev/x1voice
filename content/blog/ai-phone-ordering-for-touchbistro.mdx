If you run TouchBistro, one question decides whether a voice agent is worth the line item: does what happened on the call end up in TouchBistro correctly? Voice quality is what gets demoed and what matters least. An agent that sounds warm and drops a modifier costs you a remake, a comp, and a guest, which is a worse outcome than the call you never answered.

Here is what the connection actually does, where it fails, and what to test before you commit.

## How the order gets from the call into TouchBistro

X1 Voice reaches TouchBistro through Deliverect, the middleware many restaurants already run to bring third-party orders into their POS. The agent reads your live menu with its modifiers and prices, holds the conversation against that menu, and writes the completed order back as a ticket that fires like your other digital orders.

The round trip is the product. A system that converses beautifully and then hands a server a transcript to key in during a turn has moved the work rather than removed it, which is the case made in [why POS integration depth matters more than voice quality](/blog/why-pos-integration-depth-matters-more-than-voice-quality). Ask any vendor to show you the ticket rather than the conversation. [The TouchBistro voice order flow](/blog/touchbistro-voice-order-flow) walks the route step by step if you want to see it before a demo.

## In a table-service room, most calls are not orders

This is the part worth being honest about, because it changes what success looks like.

If you are a full-service restaurant, your phone is not mostly people ordering food. It is people asking whether you take reservations, how long the wait is, where to park, whether the kitchen is still open, and whether you have anything a vegetarian would eat. Those calls are short, repetitive, and arrive exactly when your host is seating a party.

An agent that answers all of them well is worth more to you than one that places a lot of orders, and the metric that should move is not phone revenue. It is how often your host gets pulled off the door. [Reducing host stand phone interruptions](/blog/reduce-host-stand-phone-interruptions) is the frame that fits this kind of room better than a call-containment scoreboard.

Booking is its own question. Ordering and reservations are different systems solving different problems, and the distinction is worth reading up on in [reservations versus ordering](/blog/voice-ai-reservations-vs-ordering) before a sales call, so you can ask the specific version of the question rather than the general one.

## What a real Saturday looks like

At 7:15 your host is walking a six-top to a table with three parties waiting. The phone goes four times in the next five minutes. Two of those callers want a wait time, one wants to know about parking, and one is a pickup order.

Today, one call gets answered while a guest stands there being ignored, and three do not. With an agent running, all four are picked up on the first or second ring. Three get answers you wrote in advance. The pickup order writes into TouchBistro. Your host stays on the floor.

Multiply your calls per shift by your honest average handle time and you get the floor minutes returned. Use your own figures rather than the ones on a vendor slide, and expect a smaller number than the pitch suggests. It is still real.

## Decide the handoff before go-live

Complaints, large private-event inquiries, and any caller who asks twice for a person should reach a person. The agent should hand off cleanly rather than attempt one more clarifying question, and the escalation has to ring somewhere a human will hear it at 7:30 on a Saturday, which is a thing to test and not assume. For a room with regulars, decide explicitly which callers you never want handled by an agent at all.

## The failure mode: menu drift

Everything matches on day one because you just imported it. Then the menu moves and the phone does not follow.

A seasonal dish goes into TouchBistro under a name no caller would say out loud. The kitchen 86s something at eight every Saturday. A price rises on the terminal and the phone quote stays where it was. None of these throw an error. They arrive as a guest at your counter waiting for food you cannot make.

The fix is a rule rather than a feature. Whoever owns the TouchBistro menu owns the phone menu, and every menu change earns a test call the same week. If your specials change daily, read [real-time 86ing and menu sync](/blog/real-time-86ing-menu-sync) and keep the phone menu conservative until you have measured your own sync lag with a stopwatch.

## Payment on the call

Callers pay by card during the call for pickup and delivery, and the payment reconciles with your other tender types, so close does not gain a new manual step.

The quieter benefit is what stops happening at the host stand. No digits read back across a crowded entryway, no card number on a slip of paper by the terminal, no staff member holding a stranger's payment details in their head through a rush.

## The numbers to watch

Answered-call rate first, because it moves immediately and it is why you bought this. Order accuracy next, measured by pulling twenty phone tickets and checking them against transcripts rather than trusting a summary. Escalation reasons after that, sorted into handoffs that were correct by design and handoffs that were defects you can fix in an afternoon.

For a table-service room, add one more: how often your host had to take a call anyway. If that number is not falling, the agent is answering the phone without actually absorbing the work, and the fix is usually a handful of missing answers rather than anything structural.

## Setup, and the test that settles it

Connecting takes minutes. Being ready takes a day. You link TouchBistro through Deliverect, import the menu, set hours and phone-only rules, run test calls, then forward your number. Most single locations are live inside 24 hours, and our team will do the menu pass. Plans start at $250 per month, and the full list of systems we reach is on the [integrations page](/integrations).

Then run the only test that matters. Call the demo line and place the worst order your kitchen sees, no onions, sub the fries, add a drink, apply the promo, change the size halfway through. Look at the ticket. If it is right, everything after that is tuning. If the voice repeated it back perfectly and the ticket is wrong, you learned in five minutes what would otherwise have cost you a season of comps.
