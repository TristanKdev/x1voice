In a lot of markets, a meaningful share of a restaurant's callers are more comfortable ordering in Spanish than English. When the phone can only really handle English, those calls get shorter, more error-prone, or lost entirely — and that's revenue walking out the door for a reason that has nothing to do with the food.

A bilingual voice agent closes that gap without adding staff or a phone tree.

## Meet the caller in their language

The wrong way to do multilingual phone service is the old IVR maze: "para español, oprima dos." It's slow and it signals that the second language is an afterthought. The better model is detection — the agent recognizes the language the caller is speaking and simply responds in it. The caller talks naturally; the agent keeps up. No menu, no friction, no sense of being routed to a lesser experience.

X1 Voice supports multiple languages and can meet a Spanish-speaking caller in Spanish from the first sentence, with the same order-taking, upsells, and follow-up questions it does in English.

## Language is front-of-house; the ticket is universal

An important distinction: the conversation's language and the ticket that reaches your kitchen are separate. A caller can place the entire order in Spanish, and the [order still fires into your POS](/blog/why-pos-integration-depth-matters-more-than-voice-quality) as a clean, itemized ticket in the format your staff already reads. Your kitchen doesn't need to be bilingual for your phone to be. The language serves the guest; the POS write serves the line.

## Why this captures orders you're currently losing

The recurring industry estimate is that [roughly one in four calls goes unanswered at peak](/blog/the-real-cost-of-a-missed-restaurant-phone-call) — an estimate worth measuring for yourself. In a bilingual market, add to that the calls that are technically answered but cut short because of a language mismatch: the caller who gives up, orders less than they wanted, or hangs up to try somewhere they can be understood. A bilingual agent recovers both kinds of loss, and it does it consistently on every call, at every hour.

## Set it per location

Which languages make sense depends on who calls each store, so it's a per-location setting. A location whose callers are mostly Spanish-speaking can lead in Spanish; one with a different mix can be configured accordingly. For [multi-location groups](/blog/voice-ai-multi-location-franchise-groups), that's part of the same per-store override model that handles hours, pricing, and menu differences.

The principle is simple: your phone should be as welcoming as your dining room. If a chunk of your guests are more comfortable in Spanish, a bilingual voice agent makes sure the phone doesn't quietly turn them away — and it books those orders into the same clean pipeline as everyone else's.
