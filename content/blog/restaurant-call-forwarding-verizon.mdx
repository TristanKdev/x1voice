Forwarding a Verizon business line takes about forty seconds. Everything that goes wrong goes wrong around it.

I've watched restaurants forward the wrong line, forward a line that was already in a hunt group, and forward correctly to a system that then dumped every caller into a voicemail box nobody had checked since 2019. The mechanics are trivial. The configuration around the mechanics is where the money leaks.

## The forty-second version

Pick up the handset on the line you want to forward. Dial `*72`. Wait for the stutter dial tone. Dial the ten-digit destination number. On many Verizon lines the forward isn't confirmed until the destination answers, so have someone at the other end pick up, or the forward silently doesn't take. To turn it off, dial `*73` from the same line.

That's forward-all. Every call to that number goes straight to the destination and your handset never rings.

## Which Verizon service you have decides everything

Verizon sells more than one thing that a restaurant calls "our phone line," and the instructions differ.

### Traditional copper business lines

These are the ones where star codes work. `*72` and `*73` for forward-all. Busy-line and no-answer forwarding are separate subscribed features, commonly `*90`/`*91` and `*92`/`*93`, and they only work if Verizon has provisioned them on your account. Codes vary by state and by the vintage of the line, so confirm yours with business support rather than trusting a printout taped inside the office door since the last owner.

### Verizon Business Digital Voice

This is the hosted product, managed through a web portal and a user-level settings page. Forwarding is a toggle with a destination field, and you get forward-always, forward-busy, forward-no-answer, and usually a selective rule based on time of day or calling number. Star codes may still work on the handset, but the portal is authoritative and the two can disagree in ways that are miserable to debug. Set it in one place and leave the other alone.

If you don't know which one you have, look at the bill. A line item for a hosted seat or a bundled phone means the second. A per-line charge with feature add-ons listed separately means the first.

## Forward-all versus overflow, at 6pm

This is the decision that actually matters, and most operators make it by accident.

Forward-all means every call goes to the voice agent, always. Your staff never touch the phone. That's the right setting if the agent is genuinely handling ordering and transferring the calls that need a person back to you, and it's what most restaurants land on once they trust the system.

No-answer forwarding means the phone rings in your restaurant for a set number of rings, and only the calls nobody grabs roll to the agent. It sounds safer and it usually performs worse, because those four rings are dead air to a caller who is already deciding whether to try the next place. It also produces a confusing metric picture, since your busiest calls become the ones that look like overflow. If you're overflowing rather than forwarding, at least drop the ring count to two or three.

A third option is forwarding only outside your open hours, which is the conservative way to start and is laid out in [after-hours phone answering](/blog/24-7-restaurant-phone-answering-after-hours).

Whichever you choose, the agent still needs a transfer path back into the building. That path can't be the same number you forwarded, or you'll build a loop. Give it a second line, a cell, or a direct extension.

## Five things that break

- **The hunt group.** If your main number rolls to a second and third line when busy, forwarding only the main number does nothing for the calls that already rolled. Every line in the group needs to point at the agent, or the group's own overflow destination needs changing.
- **Voicemail wins.** Verizon voicemail on the destination side, or a no-answer forward that fires before the agent picks up, will send callers to a mailbox instead. Test with a real inbound call and listen to what actually answers.
- **The credit card terminal.** Older setups share a line with a card terminal or an alarm dialer. Forwarding that line breaks the device, usually quietly, and you find out at close.
- **The wrong line.** Restaurants routinely have a number printed on the menu that isn't the number Verizon lists as the main. Forward the one customers dial, which you confirm by calling it from outside.
- **The unattended handset.** With forward-all active, a staff member picking up the restaurant phone hears nothing unusual, so nobody notices the setting was reversed by a service visit until orders stop arriving.

## Voicemail is the quiet failure

Worth being blunt: forwarding into a system that ends at voicemail is not an improvement over a ringing phone. It's slightly worse, because the caller now believes they left a message with you.

Before you finalize anything, call your restaurant number from a cell you've never called from, and let the whole thing play out. Then call it a second time and stay silent for thirty seconds to see what the system does when it hears nothing. Then call from a blocked number. Those three calls surface almost every misconfiguration people spend a week chasing.

## Forwarding is not porting

Forwarding leaves your number with Verizon and sends the calls onward. Porting moves the number itself. Start with forwarding, because it's reversible in one dialed code and requires no paperwork. Consider porting later, once you've run for a month and know the system holds up.

The tradeoff is real either way, and the details of moving a restaurant number without losing calls are in [porting your restaurant phone number](/blog/phone-number-porting-guide-restaurants). ## Run this test before Friday

Turn the forward on at 2pm on a slow Tuesday, not at 5pm on a Friday. Then place four calls from outside the building: a simple pickup order, a question about hours, a request for a person, and one where you hang up mid-sentence. Check the POS after each.

If all four behave, leave it running through one dinner service and read the call log the next morning before you touch anything else. If the log shows calls you can't account for, the hunt group is the first place to look, not the agent.
