A customer looks up your restaurant at 6:35 on a Friday, taps the number on your Google listing, and hears four rings, a pleasant recorded greeting with your restaurant's name, and then a beep. They don't leave a message. They tap back and call the place two blocks over.

Nothing malfunctioned. That is Grasshopper working exactly as designed. It routed the call to a business greeting and then to voicemail, because the phones it forwards to were busy or in someone's apron pocket next to a slicer.

## What Grasshopper is for

Grasshopper is a virtual phone number layered on top of phones you already own. You pick a local, toll-free, or vanity number, you record greetings, you set up a couple of extensions, and calls forward to cell phones. Voicemail arrives as audio plus a transcript. There's an app so you can place outbound calls that display the business number instead of your personal one.

That's a genuinely useful product for a certain shape of business. A contractor, a consultant, a small agency: one or two people who are the business, who can call back in an hour without losing the job, and who mostly need to keep their personal number private. It's built for exactly that and it does it well.

A restaurant is a different shape. Your calls arrive in clusters during the two hours when every person on your payroll has both hands occupied, and the value of a call decays to roughly zero within about fifteen minutes of it being placed. Those two facts break the model, not because the software is bad but because the software's job ends where your problem begins.

## The line between routing and answering

This is the whole comparison in one sentence: Grasshopper decides where a call goes, and a voice agent decides what happens on the call.

A voice agent picks up, greets the caller, answers whether you have gluten-free crust, takes the order with modifiers and a special instruction, quotes a pickup time based on your actual hours, and writes the ticket into your POS. If the call is a complaint or a fifty-person catering inquiry, it hands the caller to a person. If it's a job applicant, it takes a message. The call ends resolved rather than deferred.

Grasshopper cannot do any of that, and it doesn't claim to. Comparing them is only useful once you're clear that the products sit on opposite sides of the ring.

## When a virtual number is the right call

A voice agent is not the answer for every restaurant, and it's worth being blunt about where it isn't.

- Your phone rings a few times a day and someone always gets it. Adding automation solves a problem you don't have.
- You're a reservation-only room where the phone conversation is part of what guests are paying for, and a person should be having it.
- You're pre-opening and just need a number on the permit paperwork and the sign.
- Your call volume is high but almost entirely from third-party couriers and vendors rather than customers, in which case fix the routing before you fix the answering.
- You haven't measured your miss rate yet, and you're about to buy a solution to a problem you've assumed rather than counted.

That last one is where most operators actually are. Before comparing products, spend one dinner rush counting how many calls ring out. [What a missed restaurant phone call costs](/blog/the-real-cost-of-a-missed-restaurant-phone-call) walks through turning that count into a dollar figure with your own average ticket, and the arithmetic is simple enough to do on the back of a prep list.

## Running both, at least for a while

You do not have to choose immediately. Keep the Grasshopper number as your published line, because it's on your menus and your listings, and change its forwarding destination from a cell phone to your agent's number.

Two things to fix when you do. Turn off Grasshopper's voicemail on that path, or you'll create a race between the forward connecting and the mailbox picking up, and some orders will lose. And remove every other forwarding destination, so you don't have a cell phone and an agent both answering the same call while your order log develops gaps.

There's also a greeting question worth thinking about. Grasshopper can play a recorded greeting before it forwards, and it's tempting to keep the one you already recorded because it has your restaurant's name in it. Delete it anyway. The agent greets the caller itself, and stacking a recording in front means every customer hears your name twice and waits an extra several seconds to say anything. A caller who has to sit through two introductions before ordering a pizza notices, even if they can't articulate what annoyed them.

Confirm what caller ID arrives at the agent, too. Some forwarding setups present the original caller's number and some substitute the business number, and callbacks on a dropped order depend on getting the real one.

Once you've decided the agent is staying, port the number directly onto the answering platform. Forwarding adds a ring cycle to every call and a second platform that can have an outage independently of the first. If the number is a vanity or memorable local number you've been advertising for years, hold onto it and move it, which is the entire point of [vanity phone numbers for restaurants](/blog/vanity-phone-numbers-restaurants) and of choosing between [a local and a toll-free number](/blog/local-vs-toll-free-number-restaurants).

## What you're comparing on cost

These are not products priced in the same range, and any comparison that treats them as alternatives on a spreadsheet row is misleading. A virtual number costs about what a couple of streaming subscriptions cost. X1 Voice plans start at $250 a month. Check current pricing for anything else you're considering rather than trusting a number in an article.

The honest way to compare them is against the revenue at stake rather than against each other. Take the calls you miss in a week, multiply by your average off-premise ticket, and see what that annualizes to. If the number is small, keep the virtual number and spend your money elsewhere. If four missed calls a night at a $35 ticket is a real hole in your week, then you're not choosing between two phone products. You're choosing between a number that rings and a number that gets answered, and the second one has to pay for itself out of orders you're currently sending to a competitor. That comparison is also the frame for [AI ordering versus an answering service](/blog/ai-phone-ordering-vs-answering-services), which is the other product restaurants weigh at this stage.

Count first. The count decides this, not the feature list.
