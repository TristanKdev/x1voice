If your restaurant's number displays as "Spam Likely," "Scam Likely," or a similar warning, you've lost the ability to reach your own customers. The calls that get flagged are the ones you most need answered: an order problem, a delivery that can't find the address, a catering confirmation. Customers don't answer flagged calls, and most never find out what you wanted.

The realistic path is: confirm the flag exists and where, understand what caused it, fix the underlying behavior, and then submit for remediation to the analytics providers carriers rely on. It's a process with a lag, not a switch.

## First, confirm it's actually happening

Don't act on a single report. Labeling varies by carrier and by the app on the recipient's phone, so one customer saying "it said spam" might be one carrier's analytics and not a universal problem.

How to check properly:

- Call several people who do not have your number saved, across different carriers, and ask exactly what appeared on screen.
- Check whether the issue is a spam label specifically, or simply no business name showing. Those are different problems with different fixes — the name display side is covered in [restaurant caller ID and branded calling](/blog/restaurant-caller-id-branded-calling).
- Some call analytics providers offer lookup tools where you can check how a number is scored. Your phone provider may also be able to tell you what they see.

Ten minutes here saves you from spending money remediating a problem you don't have.

## Understand what likely caused it

Spam labeling is generally driven by analytics companies scoring calling patterns and incorporating user reports. The mechanisms aren't fully public, and any vendor claiming exact knowledge of the algorithms is overstating. But the commonly understood contributing factors are worth knowing:

**Call patterns that resemble telemarketing.** A burst of outbound calls in a short window, especially to numbers that don't answer or that hang up quickly, looks statistically like a dialer.

**Short call durations.** Lots of very short calls is a pattern associated with spam operations.

**Recipient reports.** If customers mark your calls as spam — which happens when they don't recognize the number — that feeds back into the scoring.

**Inherited reputation.** Phone numbers get recycled. A number that previously belonged to a telemarketer can arrive already carrying a poor reputation, and the new owner has no idea until customers stop answering.

**Number pools.** If calls on your behalf go out from a shared pool of numbers used by many businesses, the pool's collective behavior affects reputation.

That last one is worth checking if a vendor places calls or texts for you. Ask what number outbound communications originate from.

## Fix the underlying behavior first

Remediation without a behavior change tends to be temporary. Before submitting anything:

- **Reduce unnecessary outbound calls.** Much of what restaurants call about — order ready, running late, substitution needed — works better as a text anyway, and texts don't have this problem. See [restaurant order status text updates](/blog/restaurant-order-status-text-updates).
- **Avoid burst dialing.** If you're calling a list of catering customers or reservation holders, spreading it out is better than a rapid sequence.
- **Make sure customers recognize you.** A customer who knows the number is your restaurant doesn't report it. Putting your phone number on confirmations and receipts helps here, as does registering your business name so it displays.
- **Check what your vendors do on your behalf.** If a system places calls from your number, its patterns are now your reputation.

## Then pursue remediation

The practical route is to work with the analytics providers whose scoring carriers use, and with your own phone provider.

Steps that generally apply:

1. **Start with your carrier or VoIP provider.** Many have a process for reporting mislabeled business numbers, and they know which analytics providers matter for their network. This is the right first call.
2. **Register your number as a legitimate business** with the major call analytics providers. Several offer free registration or correction portals for businesses. Search for the current process directly rather than going through a reseller promising results.
3. **Register your caller name** so your business displays properly, which reduces future spam reports from customers who don't recognize you.
4. **Be patient and re-check.** Corrections propagate across carriers on their own schedule. Re-test with the same method you used to confirm the problem, a few weeks later.

Be skeptical of anyone promising guaranteed or immediate removal. The systems aren't under any single vendor's control, and that promise isn't one they can keep.

## Should you just get a new number?

Occasionally the right answer, usually not. Your number is on packaging, signage, your Google listing, delivery platforms, third-party directories you don't control, and saved in thousands of customer contacts. Changing it means a long tail of customers calling a dead line, and you'll never fully update every place it appears.

It's worth considering only if the number came to you already poisoned by a previous owner and remediation has genuinely failed. If you do change numbers, keep the old one forwarding for a long time. The mechanics of moving numbers are in [the phone number porting guide for restaurants](/blog/phone-number-porting-guide-restaurants).

And note: a new number won't stay clean if the calling pattern that caused the flag continues.

## Reduce your dependence on outbound calls

The most durable fix is needing fewer callbacks. Most restaurant outbound calls are information delivery, and information delivers fine by text. Order confirmations, ready notifications, delay updates, and substitution options can all go out as messages the customer reads on their own schedule.

That leaves calls for the genuinely interactive situations — a driver who can't find an address, a catering order that needs discussion — which is a small enough volume that answer rates matter less and pattern-based flagging is less likely.

## The bottom line

A spam label costs you the ability to reach customers about the things that matter most, and it usually comes from a mix of calling patterns, customer reports, and sometimes an inherited reputation you never earned. Confirm the problem across multiple carriers before spending anything. Fix the behavior first — fewer bursts, fewer unnecessary calls, a recognizable business name — then work through your carrier and the major analytics providers to get the number corrected, expecting a lag rather than an instant fix. Treat a number change as a last resort given how many places your number lives. And shift what you can to text, because the best-answered callback is the one you never had to make.
