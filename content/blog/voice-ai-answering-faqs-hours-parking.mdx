A large share of the calls your restaurant takes will never become orders. They're questions: are you open, where do I park, do you deliver to my neighborhood, is my food ready. Each one takes thirty seconds and costs essentially nothing.

Except they don't arrive one at a time. They cluster at exactly the hours when a line and a staff member are scarcest, and the ninth "what time do you close" during a Friday rush is a genuine tax on your ability to take orders. That's the case for handling them automatically — not that any individual question call is a problem, but that in aggregate they compete with revenue.

## The questions that repeat

If you listened to a week of your own calls, most of the non-order volume would fall into a short list:

- **Are you open / what are your hours?** The most common by a wide margin, and it spikes around holidays and weather.
- **Where are you / how do I get there?** Especially for locations that are hard to find or in a complex.
- **Where do I park?** Underrated. In dense areas this is a real decision factor for whether someone comes at all.
- **Do you deliver to me?** Which is its own [surprisingly messy topic](/blog/delivery-zone-rules-phone-orders).
- **Is my order ready?** The callback tax, addressed more directly by [texting status updates](/blog/restaurant-order-status-text-updates).
- **Do you have [dish]? Is it gluten free? Do you have vegetarian options?**
- **Do you take reservations / how big a party can you seat?**
- **Are you hiring?**

The first six are well-suited to automation because the answer space is narrow and stable. The last two are worth thinking about separately.

## Why these are the easiest calls to automate well

This category is where voice AI is at its most reliable, and it's worth understanding why: the answer doesn't require judgment. "We close at 10" is either right or wrong, and it's right if your hours are configured correctly. There's no menu mapping, no modifier structure, no POS write. Almost the entire risk is in the configuration rather than the conversation.

That's a meaningfully different reliability profile from order-taking, where the system has to interpret ambiguous speech against a complex menu. If you're skeptical about AI taking orders, question-answering is the part of the job you should be least skeptical about — the same distinction we draw in [can AI actually take restaurant phone orders](/blog/can-ai-take-restaurant-phone-orders).

## Parking deserves its own paragraph

It seems minor and isn't, particularly for urban restaurants. "Where do I park" is often the last question between someone and deciding to come to you, and the honest answer is frequently complicated: a lot behind the building, street parking after 6, a garage half a block down that validates.

None of that fits on a website header, and staff answer it inconsistently because everyone knows a different subset. Writing it down once, properly, so it's answered the same way every time is a small win that's genuinely worth the ten minutes. It also has a side effect worth noting: it forces you to actually decide what the answer is.

## The questions that need care

Two categories where automatic answering deserves more thought.

**Allergen and dietary questions.** "Is the pasta gluten free" looks like a factual lookup and isn't, because the real answer usually involves shared fryers, shared surfaces, and preparation practices that change with who's on the line. A phone agent can state what's on the menu; it shouldn't be making binding safety promises. We cover the reasoning in [allergen and dietary questions](/blog/voice-ai-allergen-dietary-questions), and the safe pattern is to answer factually about ingredients and route anything about severity or cross-contact to a person.

**Reservations and party size.** If you don't take reservations, saying so cleanly is easy. If you do, this is a different system with its own state, and the honest boundary is covered in [reservations vs. ordering](/blog/voice-ai-reservations-vs-ordering).

## Configuration is where this succeeds or fails

Since the risk lives in the configuration rather than the conversation, the work is unglamorous: write the answers down, correctly, once.

- Hours, including delivery hours if they differ, and last-order times.
- Holiday overrides, kept current. See [holiday hours overrides](/blog/holiday-hours-overrides-voice-ai).
- Parking, in specifics.
- Directions, especially any non-obvious detail — "we're behind the bank, not on the main road."
- Delivery boundary and minimum.
- The handful of menu questions you get constantly.

Then call your own number monthly and ask five of them. Configuration drifts, and the only reliable way to find out is to be a customer occasionally.

## The quiet benefit

Beyond freeing your line, there's a second effect worth naming. Question calls are frequently handled worst by human staff during a rush — clipped, distracted, occasionally wrong, because the person answering is expediting food with the phone on their shoulder. A caller asking whether you're open on Christmas Eve gets a confident guess from someone who hasn't seen the schedule.

A configured answer is consistent by construction. It's right every time, or wrong every time — and wrong every time is at least a problem you can find and fix, which is more than can be said for wrong-when-busy.

## The bottom line

Non-order calls are individually trivial and collectively expensive, and they're the easiest thing for a voice agent to handle reliably because the answers are configuration rather than judgment. Write down your hours, parking, directions, and delivery boundary properly, be careful with allergen questions, keep holiday overrides current, and call your own number occasionally to check. It's the lowest-risk part of automating your phone and one of the more useful.
