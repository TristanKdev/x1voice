A corporate assistant calls at 4:15 on a Tuesday. She needs lunch for 40 on Thursday, has an $18-per-head ceiling, two vegetarians, one gluten-free, and a loading dock that closes at 11. Your chef is deep in prep for a Wednesday event and the phone rings out. She calls the next kitchen on her list, which answers.

That call was worth somewhere north of $700 in food alone, and unlike a walked table you will never find out it happened. A catering-only kitchen has no dining room to absorb the loss and no walk-in traffic to make up the difference. The phone is the front door, and it is often unstaffed for hours at a time because everyone who could answer it is cooking.

## Your call mix looks nothing like a restaurant's

A pizza shop's phone is almost entirely transactions. Someone wants a large pepperoni, the call lasts ninety seconds, and the outcome is an order in the POS.

A catering kitchen's phone is almost entirely inquiries. The caller wants to know if you can do 60 people on the 14th, whether you deliver to their side of town, what a boxed-lunch package runs per head, and whether you can work around a nut allergy. Very few of those calls end in a completed order on the first contact. Most of them end in a file that either gets followed up on or doesn't.

That difference changes what a voice agent is for. In a restaurant, the measure is orders captured. In your kitchen, the measure is qualified leads captured with enough detail that the callback is productive. If your catering manager has to call back and open with "so, tell me what you're looking for," the agent did not do its job.

## What the agent should collect, and what it should not touch

The useful version of this is narrow and specific. On an inbound inquiry, the agent works through a short set of facts and gets off the phone.

- Headcount and whether that number is firm or an estimate, because a "roughly 50" is a different conversation than a confirmed 50.
- The date and the service time, plus whether the food needs to be hot on arrival or can be dropped cold.
- Delivery address with the access detail: dock hours, elevator, whether there is a person to receive it, what floor.
- Dietary restrictions stated as counts, not as categories. "Three vegetarian, one celiac" is actionable. "Some dietary stuff" is not.
- A callback number and the caller's role, since an assistant booking for a partner has a different decision path than the person paying.

What it should not do is quote a custom job. Publishing your standard per-head package prices through the agent is fine, and so is stating your minimums and your delivery radius. Beyond that, a number said out loud on the phone is a number you will be expected to honor, and catering margins do not survive many of those. Route pricing conversations that involve staffing, rentals, bar service, or menu design to your catering manager. We go through the qualification structure in more depth in [catering lead qualification](/blog/catering-lead-qualification-ai).

## Dead hours are where the money actually is

Restaurants worry about the dinner rush. Your problem is the opposite shape.

Corporate buyers plan during their workday, and their workday includes the hours when your kitchen is either empty or in the middle of a load-out. School and church coordinators call on evenings and weekends. A wedding planner calls when the couple gets back to them, which is whenever. None of those windows line up neatly with someone sitting near your phone.

Right now those calls hit voicemail, and a meaningful share of people planning an event will not leave one. They are calling three kitchens and going with whoever picks up first. An agent that answers at 8pm on a Sunday and captures the headcount, date, and callback number turns a lost inquiry into a Monday-morning callback where you already know what they want. The [after-hours answering case](/blog/24-7-restaurant-phone-answering-after-hours) is stronger for catering than for almost any other format.

## Deposits and contracts stay with a person

Do not let an agent take a deposit or agree to terms.

Deposit collection over the phone is a payment conversation with a compliance dimension, and it is also the moment where the client is deciding whether you are a serious operation. Both of those argue for a human voice. The agent's role is to tell the caller what your deposit terms are, if you publish them, and to move the call forward. Our note on [deposits for large catering](/blog/deposits-for-large-catering) covers the structure most kitchens land on.

The same applies to anything contractual: cancellation windows, guaranteed counts, service-staff arrangements. State the policy, do not negotiate it.

## Standing orders are the easy half

Repeat business is the part that automates cleanly. An office that orders the same 25-person sandwich spread every Wednesday is placing an order, not an inquiry, and if that package exists as items in your POS the agent can take it end to end.

This matters more than it sounds, because standing orders are the calls most likely to get bumped. Your catering manager is on with a new lead worth $3,000, and the regular $400 Wednesday order goes to voicemail. The agent takes the routine one so the person handles the one that needs a person. That split is most of the practical value.

If you run catering off a separate line from a restaurant operation, keep the routing clean. A [second phone line for catering](/blog/second-phone-line-for-catering) with its own greeting and its own escalation rules will behave better than one agent trying to serve both call types.

## Whether the volume justifies it

Be honest about the arithmetic rather than assuming low call count means low stakes.

Take your average job value over the last quarter. Take the number of inquiry calls you get in a week and estimate, conservatively, what share currently reach voicemail during prep, service, or after hours. Multiply the missed inquiries by your historical close rate and by average job value. A kitchen closing one in four inquiries at an average of $900 loses $225 in expected revenue every time an inquiry goes unanswered, which means missing one a day is real money against a plan that [starts at $250 a month](/pricing).

If that math comes out small, say so and skip it. Some kitchens genuinely run on three long-standing corporate accounts and a referral pipeline, and the phone is nearly decorative. But most catering operators have never counted their missed inquiries, and the count is usually higher than the guess.

One test settles it this week. Forward your catering line to your cell for five days and write down every call that comes in outside the hours someone is normally at the desk. Not the calls you missed during service, just the ones that arrive when the building is empty. That list is what an agent would have caught, and it will tell you within a week whether this is a real problem at your operation or a solved one.
