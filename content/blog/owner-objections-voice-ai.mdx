An owner I talked with last spring had the same reaction most owners have. He listened to a demo, nodded through it, and then said: "Yeah, but my customers are going to hate it."

That is objection number four on this list, and it happens to be the one where he was partly right. The rest of them have cleaner answers. Here are the six that come up in almost every conversation, with the honest version of each, including where the objection lands and the vendor should admit it.

## "It costs too much for a place my size"

This is arithmetic, not opinion, and it is worth doing on paper before anything else.

X1 Voice starts at $250 a month. Your side of the equation is the number of calls you currently do not answer, times your average ticket, times the share of those callers who would have ordered. Count the first number for one week using your phone system's logs. Do not estimate it.

If you miss twelve calls a week and your average takeout ticket is $32, and you assume half of those callers ordered somewhere else instead of calling back, that is roughly $190 a week in orders that left. Over a month that clears the subscription with room. If you miss two calls a week, it does not, and you should not buy this. We would rather tell you that now. The full version of the calculation is in [the real cost of a missed restaurant phone call](/blog/the-real-cost-of-a-missed-restaurant-phone-call), and current pricing across the category is in [what AI phone answering costs](/blog/ai-phone-answering-cost-2026).

## "It will not understand my customers"

Half true, and the half that is true is specific.

Speech recognition in 2026 handles ordinary accented English well. Where it still degrades is heavy background noise, speakerphone in a car, callers who speak very quietly, and menu items with names that do not resemble English words. That last one is not really a speech problem. It is a menu configuration problem, and it is fixable by teaching the system the variants your customers actually say.

The mistake is accepting a vendor's accuracy claim from a demo recorded in a quiet room. Your test is your own line, with your own callers, for two weeks. Then read the transcripts of the escalated calls and count how many failed for a reason that could be fixed versus a reason that could not. [Accents and speech recognition accuracy](/blog/voice-ai-accent-speech-recognition-accuracy) covers what to look for.

## "What happens when it gets stuck?"

The right answer is that it hands the call to a person, and that you decide when.

This is a configuration decision, not a technical limitation, and it is the one operators most often leave on the default. Write down the categories that should always reach a human: complaints, anything involving a refund, catering above a dollar threshold, and any caller who asks for a person. That last rule should be absolute, and an agent that argues with a caller who wants a human is doing real damage while its metrics look fine.

The other half of this objection is what happens when the internet drops or the system goes down mid-service. Ask the vendor exactly where calls go in that case. "It rolls to your existing line" is an acceptable answer. Silence is not. [Human handoff and failover](/blog/human-handoff-failover-voice-ai) walks through both paths.

## "My customers will hate it"

Some of them will. That is the honest answer, and any vendor who tells you otherwise is selling.

The realistic version: most callers placing a straightforward takeout order do not care, because they want the food and the interaction is transactional. A smaller group notices, dislikes it, and orders anyway. A small group will hang up. And your regulars, the people who call weekly and expect to be recognized, are the ones with a real grievance, which is why [what happens to your regulars](/blog/regulars-and-ai-phone-answering) deserves its own decision.

What moves this from a risk to a manageable one is a clean path to a person and an agent that identifies itself honestly. What makes it worse is a system that traps people. There is more on the caller-reaction question in [will customers hate an AI answering your phone](/blog/will-customers-hate-ai-answering).

## "My staff will think I am replacing them"

They will think it, and the reason they think it is that you have not told them what actually changes.

The version that lands with a crew is concrete. The phone stops interrupting the host during a seating rush. Nobody has to take an order with a headset on while ringing up a walk-in. Nobody loses hours. If you are in fact planning to cut hours, say that too, because they will work it out within a month and the discovery is worse than the news.

Run this conversation before the install, not after. [Handling staff pushback](/blog/staff-pushback-voice-ai) has the specific script, including what to do with the person who is genuinely angry about it.

## "I will be stuck with the vendor"

This one is milder than it feels, and it has one real edge.

Your menu lives in your POS, not in the voice platform, so switching does not mean rebuilding your menu from scratch. X1 Voice reads from Square, Clover and OrderCounter directly, and reaches Toast, Lightspeed, TouchBistro, SpotOn, Aloha, Revel, PAR Brink and Micros through Deliverect, which means the connection is the thing you replace rather than the data.

The part you can genuinely lose is call history. Transcripts and recordings live with the vendor, and some contracts do not commit to giving them back. Ask three questions before signing: what happens to my recordings if I cancel, in what format do I get them, and how long do you keep them afterward. Get it in writing. The mechanics of a move are in [switching voice AI vendors](/blog/switching-voice-ai-vendors).

Six objections, and two of them, cost and customer reaction, are legitimate enough that they should sometimes stop the purchase. If your phone rings a handful of times a day, or your business runs on regulars who call by name, the answer may simply be no. Count your missed calls for one week first. That single number resolves more of this than any demo will.
