A travel weekend is not automatically a busy weekend for your phone. For plenty of restaurants it is the quietest three days of the month, and the operators who staff up for it are paying for coverage they do not need while the ones who should have staffed up are drowning.

Which one you are depends on a single question: do your regular customers leave town for Memorial Day, or do other people's regular customers arrive in yours?

## Two restaurants, opposite problems, same weekend

A waterfront place, a restaurant near a state park, anything on a route people drive to get somewhere else: your call volume goes up, and the mix shifts hard toward questions from people who have never eaten with you. They found you on a map, they are in a car with four other people, and they want to know if you have a table and where to park.

A downtown lunch counter serving an office district, a strip-center spot in a commuter suburb: your Friday is soft, your Saturday and Sunday might be fine, and Monday can be dead. The customers who normally call you at eleven forty-five are at a lake.

Both are ordinary. What causes trouble is applying one restaurant's holiday plan to the other. Staffing an extra host for a phone that rings twelve times all day is a wasted shift. Running the usual skeleton crew on a weekend when the phone rings two hundred times is a lot of business you never learned you lost.

## Find out which one you are before you plan anything

The answer is in last year's data, not in a general sense of how holidays go.

Pull call volume for the same weekend last year, and the year before if you have it. Compare each day to the four ordinary weekends around it. You are looking for direction and magnitude, and you will usually get an obvious answer within about five minutes of looking.

If your phone system does not retain call records, that is the finding. Not knowing whether your busiest hours produce fifteen calls or a hundred and fifty is a real gap, and it affects far more than one weekend. Most operators who look for the first time are surprised in one direction or the other, which is the whole argument in [call volume forecasting for restaurants](/blog/call-volume-forecasting-restaurants).

Then adjust for what you know changed. A new hotel two blocks away, a competitor that closed, a bridge under construction that reroutes traffic past you or away from you. Local knowledge beats trend lines here, because the swing is caused by specific physical things.

## Visitor calls are cheap to answer and expensive to miss

If you are on the busy side of this, look at what the calls actually contain. Very few of them are orders. Almost all of them are a question you have answered a thousand times.

Are you open Monday. Is there parking. How long is the wait right now. Can we bring a dog. Do you take reservations for six. Is the kitchen open until close or does it stop earlier.

Every one of those takes under a minute and produces nothing on its own, and every one of them is a party deciding where to spend a hundred and fifty dollars in the next twenty minutes. A call that rings out is a party that goes to the next result on their phone. Unlike a walked table, you never learn it happened, which is why this loss stays invisible year after year. The arithmetic of that is laid out in [the real cost of a missed restaurant phone call](/blog/the-real-cost-of-a-missed-restaurant-phone-call).

The saving grace is that these are the most automatable calls that exist. They have fixed answers, they do not require judgment, and they do not touch your POS. A phone that answers hours, parking, and wait-time questions without pulling anyone off the floor solves most of a travel weekend, and it is the narrow case where automation is unambiguously the right tool. [Voice AI answering FAQs about hours and parking](/blog/voice-ai-answering-faqs-hours-parking) covers what that setup looks like.

## Adding a person to the host stand does not fix the phone

The usual response to a busy holiday is to schedule one more body at the door. It rarely works for phone coverage, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of effort.

Whoever stands at that podium is looking at guests physically in front of them. When a party of five walks in and the phone rings at the same moment, the guests win, correctly. So the extra person you added to answer the phone spends the shift seating tables, and the phone is exactly where it was before, except your labor cost went up.

If you want a person on the phone, the role needs a station that is not the door and a manager willing to protect it, which on a holiday weekend is a lot to ask. Otherwise you are choosing between accepting missed calls and moving the calls off the floor entirely. Both are legitimate choices. Pretending you made the first one when you actually made neither is what most restaurants do. There is a fuller version of that math in [peak hour staffing math for the phone](/blog/peak-hour-staffing-math-phone), and the broader constraint in [staffing shortages and phone coverage](/blog/restaurant-staffing-shortage-phone-coverage).

## If volume drops instead, spend the weekend differently

For the restaurants that go quiet, the useful move is not cost-cutting for three days. It is using the low-stakes window to fix things you cannot touch when you are full.

A slow Saturday is when you verify that your holiday hours are right everywhere, test how your phone handles the questions a visitor would ask, retrain whoever is newest on taking a large order cleanly, and check that your menu availability actually matches what the kitchen is running. If you are considering automating any of the phone, this is the week to hear it on real calls at low volume rather than turning it on cold in front of a Fourth of July rush six weeks later.

That is the honest sequencing advice. Start new phone tooling on a quiet weekend and let it be boring. Whatever is wrong with your hours, your menu names, or your escalation rules will surface on ordinary calls, and you will have time to fix it before it matters.

## The number to write down on Tuesday

Whichever direction your weekend went, record two figures before the week absorbs them: total calls per day, and your best honest estimate of how many went unanswered during your single busiest hour.

Next year you will have something to plan against instead of an argument about whether it felt busy. Three years of that and Memorial Day stops being a guess, which is worth more than any single decision you make about it this year.
