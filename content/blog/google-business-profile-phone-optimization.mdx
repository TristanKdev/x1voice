A guest searches for pho near them at 8:40 on a Tuesday. Three listings come up on the map. Two show a call button, current hours, and a photo of a bowl. The third shows a category of "Restaurant," hours that ended at 8:00, and a stock exterior shot. Nobody taps the third one, and its owner never learns that the search happened.

That is where most of the decision gets made now. Not on your website. On a card in a map result, in about four seconds, on a phone that is already in call mode.

## The call button is the actual product

On mobile, your listing renders a call button next to directions. Tapping it dials you directly, with no page load in between. For a restaurant, that button is the single highest-intent surface you own, because the person pressing it has already decided and only wants to place an order or check something small before deciding.

Which reframes the whole optimization question. You are not trying to get more website visits from your listing. You are trying to get more taps on that button, and then to actually be there when it rings. The second half is where restaurants lose the return on all of this, and it is quantified in [the real cost of a missed call](/blog/the-real-cost-of-a-missed-restaurant-phone-call).

## The fields that move call volume

### Primary category

This is the field that changes the most and the one most commonly wrong. Primary category determines which searches you are eligible for. "Restaurant" is the default and it is nearly useless, because it puts you in a pool with every other place that serves food.

Pick the most specific category that describes what you sell most of. Pizza restaurant. Taco restaurant. Barbecue restaurant. Then add secondary categories for the real, smaller parts of your business, such as caterer or delivery service, without stuffing in categories you cannot deliver on. A listing claiming to be a caterer that cannot quote a fifty-person order generates calls you will waste time on.

### Hours, and especially the special ones

Regular hours are table stakes. Special hours are where the phone actually moves.

Google surfaces holiday hours prominently and flags listings whose holiday status is unconfirmed. Setting them for the ten or twelve days a year you deviate takes one sitting. Skipping it produces the specific failure of a full parking lot on a day you are closed, or worse, a closed-looking listing on a day you are open and staffed. If your phone coverage also changes on those days, the same calendar needs to go into your answering setup, which is the point of [holiday hours overrides](/blog/holiday-hours-overrides-voice-ai).

### The phone number itself

Put your main ordering line in the primary phone field. Not a mobile number, not the manager's cell, not a number that rolls to voicemail after hours.

The after-hours behavior of that number is part of the field, even though Google has no box for it. A listing that stays visible at 10pm with a call button on it will get dialed at 10pm, whether or not anyone is in the building. If those calls hit a voicemail box nobody empties, the listing is generating demand you are declining without knowing it.

Call tracking numbers are the recurring argument. Google's documentation allows a tracking number as primary as long as the real line is listed as an additional number, so it is permitted. The cost is that your listed number now disagrees with the number on your website, your Yelp page, your delivery app listings, and every directory that scraped you five years ago. That disagreement is exactly what weakens the entity linkage described in [name, address, and phone consistency](/blog/nap-consistency-restaurants). For a single location, the attribution data is rarely worth the mess. If you are choosing what to list in the first place, [local versus toll-free numbers](/blog/local-vs-toll-free-number-restaurants) covers the tradeoff.

## The fields worth the time, ranked honestly

- Primary category, because it decides whether you appear at all
- Special hours for holidays and any week you close early, which prevents the calls and walk-ups you least want
- The ordering and menu link fields, since a working link keeps people from calling to ask what you serve, and a broken one sends them to a competitor
- Photos of food taken in the last year, because a listing with current food photos gets engaged with and an eight-year-old exterior shot does not
- The Q&A section, seeded with your own real questions before someone else answers them for you

## What people overspend time on

- Weekly Google Posts, which decay fast and rarely change a dinner decision
- Rewriting the business description repeatedly, since it appears in few surfaces and moves little
- Chasing every attribute checkbox, most of which no one filters on
- Buying citation-blast services to build hundreds of low-quality directory listings
- Any product promising to place you in the map pack through markup on your own site

## Answering the Q&A section before someone else does

The questions-and-answers block on your listing is open to the public. Anyone can post a question, and anyone can answer it, including a person who ate at a restaurant with a similar name in a different city.

The fix is to write the questions yourself, from the business account, using the six things people actually call and ask. Do you deliver here. Is there parking. Do you have gluten-free options. How far ahead do you need a catering order. Are you open on the holiday.

Two things happen. The answer a searcher sees is now yours instead of a stranger's guess, and the volume of calls asking those specific questions drops, which frees your line for the calls that turn into tickets. Those same answers should match what your website says and what your phone says, since a searcher who reads one answer on the listing and hears another on the call trusts neither.

## The number that tells you what to fix

Your profile's performance report shows how many people tapped the call button over a period. That number is demand. It is not capture, because Google counts the tap, not whether anyone said hello.

Pull that count for a month, then pull your answered calls for the same month from your phone provider. If the profile says 380 calls and your line answered 240, you have a 140-call gap, and at a typical takeout ticket that gap is worth more than everything else on this page combined.
