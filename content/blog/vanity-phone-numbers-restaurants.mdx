A customer picks up a delivery bag with 1-800-EAT-WING printed across the side. They are standing in a parking lot with a phone already in their hand, and to dial that number they have to look down at the keypad and find where the letters live. Two seconds of hesitation. Then they open a delivery app instead, because that took none.

That is the case against vanity numbers in one paragraph, and it is also unfair, because plenty of restaurants earn real money from one. The useful question is not whether vanity numbers work. It is whether the memorability you gain is worth the dialing friction you pay, and the answer changes completely depending on how people actually reach your phone.

## What you are buying is recall, not reach

A vanity number does exactly one job. It makes your number easier to remember and easier to repeat out loud. Nothing about it makes you more findable, ranks you higher anywhere, or gets you more calls on its own.

That job matters in a narrow set of places: a radio spot, a vehicle wrap, a sign on a highway, a magnet on somebody's refrigerator, a printed menu in a hotel room. In all of those, the customer encounters your number without a link to tap. They have to hold it in their head for a few seconds or a few days. Seven letters that spell a word survive that gap much better than ten digits do.

Outside those places, recall is worth almost nothing, because recall is not what is happening. Someone searches your name, sees the Google listing, and taps the call button. The digits never enter their brain.

## The dialing tax nobody measures

Here is the part vendors selling vanity numbers do not lead with. Every letter in your number is a small translation task for the caller.

Adults who grew up dialing know the keypad well enough. Younger callers frequently do not, because they have never had a reason to learn which letters sit on the 7. And even people who know it slow down. Watch someone dial a vanity number and you will see the pause.

The cost is not that they give up on the fourth letter. It is that a slower, more effortful action loses to a faster, easier one that is sitting right next to it. A delivery app icon is one tap. Your seven-letter word is a puzzle. Under any kind of hurry, the puzzle loses.

None of this shows up in your analytics, because a call that was never placed leaves no record. It is the same invisibility problem described in [the real cost of a missed restaurant phone call](/blog/the-real-cost-of-a-missed-restaurant-phone-call), except one step earlier in the chain.

## Where vanity numbers still earn their keep

Three situations, and they are more common than the skeptical case suggests.

The first is broadcast and outdoor advertising. If you are buying radio, local TV, transit ads, or billboards, you are paying for exposure to people who cannot click. A number they can hold in memory for the drive home is worth the friction it costs later.

The second is a fleet. Pizza shops, catering operations, and anyone with delivery vehicles are running rolling billboards past thousands of people a week. Digits on a car door are forgotten by the next light. A word is not.

The third is voice. If a customer asks a friend where they got the wings, "call EAT-WING" transmits perfectly in conversation. A ten-digit number does not survive being repeated by a human being.

There is a fourth case worth naming honestly: some numbers are simply pretty. A local number ending in 1000 or repeating digits gets the same recall benefit with no letter translation at all, and it usually costs less than a branded vanity string. If memorability is the goal, look at digit patterns before you look at words.

## Local beats toll-free for a single restaurant

Toll-free numbers carry an association most independent operators do not want. They read as corporate, as a call center, as somewhere other than the corner of your street. For a neighborhood restaurant, a local area code is a signal that you are the neighborhood place, which is most of what you are selling.

Toll-free also costs more, both to acquire a good vanity string and often in per-minute charges depending on how your service is set up.

The exceptions are real but specific. A catering company selling across a metro area with several area codes benefits from one number that works everywhere. A multi-unit group putting a single number on twenty vehicles has the same problem. If that is you, a toll-free vanity number is a reasonable purchase, and it pairs badly with per-location routing unless you have planned the routing first.

## The cost side, without the sales pitch

A generic local number from your phone provider is typically free or close to it. A memorable local number costs something modest. A genuinely good toll-free vanity string that spells a real word in a real category is a resale market, and the prices reflect scarcity rather than any underlying cost, so they range from a few hundred dollars to figures that should make you walk away.

The recurring costs are ordinary. It is a phone number. It rings like any other.

The cost that surprises operators is the switching cost you create for yourself. Once a vanity number is on your vehicles, your signage, your menus, your Google listing, and eight directory sites, you are married to it. Changing it later means reprinting everything and accepting that a percentage of your regulars will call a dead line for years. Before you commit, read [the phone number porting guide](/blog/phone-number-porting-guide-restaurants) so you understand what you can and cannot take with you.

## What to check before you buy one

Four things, in order:

- Whose name the number is registered in. If a marketing agency or a phone platform holds it, you are renting your own brand and you will discover this at the worst possible moment.
- Whether the word actually spells cleanly on a keypad, including any ambiguity between similar-sounding words a caller might guess wrong.
- Whether you have a plan for the digit version, because you must publish both, and the digits are what belongs on your website and in your [directory listings](/blog/nap-consistency-restaurants).
- Whether your phone can handle a successful campaign. A vanity number that works generates a call spike, and a spike that rings out is worse than no campaign at all.

That last point is the one operators skip. The number is the cheap part.

## The test worth running first

Before spending anything, count how your calls arrive today. Most phone systems and most POS reports can tell you call volume, and your Google Business Profile will tell you how many people tapped the call button on your listing. If tapped calls are the overwhelming majority, a vanity number is buying you recall in a channel you are barely using, and the money is better spent on the parts of your phone that are already leaking, such as [caller ID and branded calling](/blog/restaurant-caller-id-branded-calling) or simply answering more of what rings.

If you are about to start buying radio, wrapping vehicles, or putting a number in front of people who cannot tap it, the arithmetic reverses and a vanity number is a reasonable line item. Decide from that number, not from how the word sounds when you say it in a meeting. And if you do buy one, register it in your own business name on day one. Everything else about a phone number is fixable later. That part is not.
