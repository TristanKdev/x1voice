A toll-free number solves a problem your customers stopped having about twenty years ago. It exists so a caller does not pay long-distance charges. Nobody has paid long-distance charges from a mobile phone in a very long time, and mobile is how nearly every order call reaches you.

So the original reason is gone, and what is left is a set of secondary effects. Most of them work against a single-location restaurant.

## The economics run backwards from what people assume

On a toll-free number, the business receiving the call pays for it. That is the definition of toll-free. The caller is not charged, because you are.

The per-minute rates are small, usually a fraction of a cent to a couple of cents. That sounds like nothing until you multiply it by a Friday. Two hundred calls averaging three minutes each, plus the hold time before anyone picks up, is roughly six hundred minutes in one night. At a penny and a half, that is nine dollars for one Friday, so call it thirty to forty a month for a busy takeout phone, more if you make people wait.

Thirty dollars a month is not the reason to switch. Point it out mainly because operators assume toll-free is free, and it is the only one of the two numbers with a meter attached. On a local number the caller's plan pays, and your per-minute cost is zero.

The second-order version is worse. Any number with a per-minute cost gives you a quiet incentive to shorten calls, and shortening calls is the wrong instinct on a phone that takes orders. The call where somebody talks themselves into adding a side is the call you want to run long.

## What each number signals to the person dialing

Here is the part that actually matters, and it is not measurable in a spreadsheet.

An 800 or 888 number reads as a company. A department. Something with a queue and a menu of options and a person in a headset who does not know where the parking lot is. When somebody sees it in search results next to three competitors with local area codes, it reads as the least local option on the page, whatever the address says.

A local area code reads as the restaurant down the street. In a lot of markets the area code carries more identity than that, and callers know the exchange the same way they know the cross streets. That association is doing free work for you every time your number appears.

The exception proves how much of this is about recognition rather than digits. In a handful of metros the original area code has become a status marker, and businesses pay real money for a number in it. That is the same mechanism working in your favor for free, as long as the number you publish is the one that matches where you actually cook.

There is a mirror-image effect on outbound. When you call a customer back about a substitution or a delayed order, a toll-free number on their screen looks like a telemarketer and gets declined. A local number gets answered more often, especially once you have your business name attached to it, which is a separate piece of setup covered in [registering your caller ID name](/blog/cnam-caller-id-name-registration) and, for the newer version that reaches mobile phones, [branded calling](/blog/restaurant-caller-id-branded-calling).

That outbound difference is the strongest practical argument, because a callback that goes unanswered turns into a wrong order or a refund.

## Where toll-free is still the right call

Say plainly when the answer flips, because it does.

Toll-free earns its keep when you sell beyond your own metro area. A restaurant shipping barbecue or sauce nationally, a catering operation working an entire state, a small group with locations in three area codes that wants one number on the truck: those are real cases. The toll-free number stops being a signal about locality and starts being a single memorable front door.

It also earns its keep as a vanity number. A word that spells your business is easier to remember than any seven digits, and the toll-free ranges are where most available vanity combinations live. If a genuinely good one is available and your marketing runs on radio or vehicle wraps, that is worth something.

And there is a narrow case for using toll-free as a tracking number, since a separate line lets you attribute calls to a specific campaign or a specific delivery app listing without touching your main number. That works, though a local tracking number does the same job without the tolls.

One more honest case: a second number of any kind is useful when a distinct part of your business deserves its own front door. A catering line that rings somewhere different, keeps its own hours, and does not compete with the dinner rush is a legitimate reason to add a number. That reason has nothing to do with whether the number is toll-free, though, and a local second line does the job identically.

The one thing none of those cases justify is putting a toll-free number in place of your local one on your Google listing. Multi-location groups that want one national number should still publish the local number for each location, because a listing is about a specific address and the phone should match it. Our [locations](/locations) approach follows the same logic.

## Running both without confusing anyone

If you already advertise toll-free, do not retire it. Numbers you have advertised keep generating calls for years from sources you have long forgotten: an old menu on a fridge, a directory nobody updates, the back of a coupon. Killing the number kills those calls silently.

Point it at the same destination as your local number. Forwarding is inexpensive, and if both numbers land in the same answering setup, the calls behave identically no matter which one the customer dialed. From there, publish only the local number going forward, and let the toll-free one decay into a redirect.

Two rules make this painless. First, both numbers should route to the same place, with the same hours, the same menu, and the same escalation policy, so a caller never gets a different experience based on which digits they happened to have. Second, whichever number you publish should be consistent everywhere at once. A restaurant with the toll-free number on its website, the local number on Google, and a third number on a delivery platform is a restaurant that cannot tell where its calls come from, and that fragmentation also confuses the systems that assemble answers about your business, which is the mechanic behind [showing up in AI search](/blog/how-restaurants-show-up-in-ai-search).

Whichever number you settle on, it is yours rather than the carrier's, and moving it between providers is a port rather than a purchase. Do that carefully and never by disconnecting first, which is the sequence in the [porting guide](/blog/phone-number-porting-guide-restaurants).

Here is the test. Look at your last month of outbound callbacks and count how many went unanswered or straight to voicemail. If that fraction is high and you are dialing out from a toll-free number, you have found a cheap fix, and it starts with dialing from a local one.
