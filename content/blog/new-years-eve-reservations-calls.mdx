A four-top calls at two in the afternoon on December 12. They want to know what the New Year's Eve menu is, whether the second seating is really at nine or closer to nine-fifteen, whether the vegetarian in the party gets the same number of courses, what the hundred and ten dollars includes, whether that is before tax and gratuity, and whether the deposit is refundable if their sitter falls through.

That is six minutes. Your host is standing at the stand with a lunch check in one hand. And there are eleven more of those calls today.

## The call is longer because the product is different

A regular reservation is a name, a time, a party size, and a phone number. A prix-fixe reservation is a purchase decision, and callers treat it like one.

They are committing a hundred dollars a head, often for a group, often on the strength of a single phone call. So they ask, and the questions are consistent enough that you can predict them almost exactly:

- What is on the menu, and can you send it to me.
- What does the price include, and is tax and service on top.
- Are seating times fixed, and how long do we have the table.
- What are the options for a vegetarian, a gluten-free guest, or a shellfish allergy.
- What is the deposit, and what happens if we cancel.

Five questions, asked in some order by nearly every caller, most of them answerable in one sentence. Which means the majority of your New Year's Eve phone time is spent repeating the same five answers to different people while a dining room needs attention.

## Answer the repeatable part at volume

This is the clearest use of a voice agent in the whole December calendar, because the questions are fixed, the answers are fixed, and the volume is the problem.

Load the five answers. Load the seating times and what remains available in each. Load the price, what it covers, and whether tax and gratuity are included, in the exact words you want said, because "included" is where disputes come from. Load the dietary paths, and be specific rather than reassuring: "there is a vegetarian version of every course" is useful, "we can accommodate most dietary needs" is not, and the second one produces an argument at the table on the 31st. The general handling of that category of question is in [allergen and dietary questions](/blog/voice-ai-allergen-dietary-questions).

Then send the menu in writing. Reading four courses aloud with substitutions is slow and the caller retains almost none of it. A text or email with the full menu, sent while they are still on the phone, does the job better and gives the party something to discuss. The confirmation mechanics are ordinary and covered in [SMS order confirmations](/blog/sms-order-confirmations-voice-ai).

Route the exceptions. Large parties, buyouts, private-room inquiries, and anyone asking about a custom menu should reach a person, because those calls are worth enough to deserve a real conversation. The distinction between reservation handling and order handling generally is worked through in [reservations vs ordering](/blog/voice-ai-reservations-vs-ordering).

## Deposits: the part that decides your night

New Year's Eve is the highest no-show risk night of the year at most restaurants, and a no-show on a prix-fixe table is not a lost cover, it is a lost hundred-plus dollars per seat on a night you turned people away.

Take a deposit. Per person, not per booking, because the party of eight that shows up as five is more common than the party that vanishes entirely.

Three things have to happen in the booking call for a deposit to actually protect you:

- The cancellation window has to be said out loud, in plain terms, and acknowledged.
- The amount and what it applies toward has to be stated before the card is taken.
- A written confirmation carrying both has to go out immediately.

A policy the guest heard and confirmed is one you can hold on December 31. A policy that exists only on your website is one you will end up waiving at the door, because arguing about it in front of a full dining room is a losing position.

On the card handling itself: the number should go to a processor and come back as a token, not sit in a transcript or on a host stand notepad. That is a compliance requirement rather than a preference, and the specifics are in [PCI compliance for restaurant phone payments](/blog/pci-compliance-restaurant-phone-payments) and [phone payment tokenization](/blog/phone-payment-tokenization-explained). The practical version of taking money over the phone without creating a problem is in [collecting payment over the phone safely](/blog/collecting-payment-over-the-phone-safely).

One more thing about the deposit conversation: it is where bookings are most often lost, and usually for a fixable reason. A caller who has spent five minutes getting excited about the menu and then hits an unexplained charge request tends to say they will call back. They do not. Sequence it so the deposit lands after the value is established and framed as what it is, a hold on a table that a dozen other people want on the same night. Said that way it reads as scarcity rather than suspicion.

## The reminder that pays for itself

Two days out, send every booking a confirmation with the date, seating time, party size, deposit amount, and cancellation deadline. Make it easy to cancel.

That last part sounds backwards. It is not. A cancellation received on December 29 is a table you resell to a waitlist that is already forming. A cancellation received at seven-thirty on the 31st is an empty four-top on the busiest night of your year. Making cancellation easy two days out converts your worst outcome into your second-best one.

## December 31 itself

The phone does not stop on the day, and what it carries changes.

Running-late calls, cancellations, parties adding or dropping a person, and a steady trickle of people calling around looking for a table anywhere. Every one of those is time-sensitive and every one of them arrives while every person you employ is on the floor. This is the day the phone gets abandoned, and it is a day where an answered call is worth an unusual amount, because a cancellation learned at six is a table sold at seven.

If you are deciding where to put your attention this December, put it here: make sure the five repeated questions get answered without a human, make sure the deposit terms are said aloud, and make sure somebody or something picks up on the 31st. The equivalent playbook for the other high-reservation holidays is in [Valentine's Day and Mother's Day reservations](/blog/valentines-day-mothers-day-reservations-calls), and the large-party specifics are in [large party reservations by phone](/blog/large-party-reservations-by-phone).

Run one test in mid-December. Have someone who does not work for you call and try to book a party of six with one vegetarian and one shellfish allergy, and pay the deposit. If they get through it in under three minutes with accurate answers and a written confirmation in hand, your New Year's Eve is already mostly handled.
