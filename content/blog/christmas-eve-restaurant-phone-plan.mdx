The common assumption about Christmas Eve is that it is a slower version of Christmas week. It is the opposite. It is the single most compressed service most restaurants run all year: a full day of demand pushed into an afternoon, with a crew that is watching the clock because they have their own families waiting.

The kitchen side of that gets planned. The phone side usually does not, and the phone is where the compression shows up first.

## What people are actually calling about

Sort a Christmas Eve call log and it separates into four piles, and only one of them is an order.

The largest pile is hours. Are you open, until when, are you doing the full menu, are you open tomorrow. These are ten-second answers that consume a real fraction of your afternoon because each one arrives at the host stand while someone is being seated.

The second pile is pickup timing on orders you already have. Callers confirming, callers running late, callers asking whether they can come early because their in-laws arrived. Every one of these needs someone to look at a ticket.

The third pile is same-day orders, which is the pile your kitchen is least equipped to absorb, and the fourth is people looking for something you do not offer, calling a list of restaurants and hoping.

Only the third pile makes you money on the day, and it is the smallest. The first two consume the most staff time and generate none. That imbalance is the whole argument for putting something on the line that answers hours and confirms tickets without pulling anyone off the floor. The general mechanics of the hours-and-parking question are in [answering FAQs about hours and parking](/blog/voice-ai-answering-faqs-hours-parking).

## Hours are the thing that breaks

Your Christmas Eve hours differ from your normal hours, and they live in at least four systems that do not talk to each other.

Google has hours. Your website has hours. Your voicemail greeting or voice agent has hours. Every delivery marketplace has its own hours, and at least one of them will still be showing you as open at nine at night when your kitchen went dark at five. That last one produces the specific and expensive failure of an order landing in your tablet after everyone has gone home.

Set special hours in early December, in every system, and then verify from the outside. Search your own restaurant, call your own number, open your own delivery listing. Do not trust the confirmation screen; trust what a customer sees. The configuration side of this for a voice agent is straightforward and is covered in [holiday hours and overrides](/blog/holiday-hours-overrides-voice-ai).

While you are in there, set the days around it too. December 25 and 26 are as likely to be misreported as the 24th, and the callers checking are checking because they are deciding where to spend money.

## Cutoffs, and holding them

The plan that fails is the one with no cutoff. Orders keep arriving, the kitchen keeps saying yes because saying no feels like turning away business, and at three in the afternoon your prep is gone and the last four orders are twenty minutes late each.

Pick a same-day cutoff. Announce it in the greeting and on your listings from December 1. Then have the phone hold it without a human having to be the bad guy, which is the part staff find hardest and software finds trivial.

Cap by pickup window rather than by day total. Your constraint on Christmas Eve afternoon is how many complete orders can be staged and handed out in a given half hour without chaos at the counter, and that number is smaller than your kitchen's cooking capacity. When a window fills, the phone should offer the next one instead of taking the order and hoping. The kitchen-capacity version of this control is described in [order throttling and kitchen capacity](/blog/order-throttling-kitchen-capacity-phone).

Hold back two windows. Something will happen, and having somewhere to put it is worth more than the two orders you did not sell.

## The order taken at 4:50 for a five o'clock close

Every Christmas Eve produces at least one of these, and it is worth naming because it is the specific incident that ruins the end of the shift.

Someone calls ten minutes before close and orders a large pickup. Whoever answers says yes, because saying yes is the reflex and because turning down a hundred dollars feels wrong. The kitchen has broken down half the line. The person who was leaving at five leaves at five-forty. And the order goes out worse than anything else you sent that day.

The way out of this is not judgment in the moment, it is a rule that predates the moment. Set a last-order time that is genuinely earlier than your close, because your close is when the door locks and your last-order time is when the line can still produce. Forty-five minutes is a reasonable gap for most kitchens and an hour is safer on a holiday with a skeleton crew.

Then let the phone enforce it. A configured agent stops taking orders at 4:15 without arguing and without feeling bad about it, and it offers the caller something instead: a pickup on the 26th, a gift card, or a note that you open at eleven on the 26th. The order you decline politely is a customer you keep. The order you accept and then execute badly at 5:35 is one you probably do not.

If you deliver, set the delivery cutoff earlier still, and confirm that your delivery marketplace listings turn off at the same hour. Those platforms hold their own schedules and an order landing on a dark tablet at 5:30 becomes a cancellation and a rating hit that lasts well past the holiday.

## The calls that arrive after you have closed

Here is the part that most operators leave on the table. Your phone rings on Christmas Eve night and all day Christmas, and for most restaurants it rings into nothing.

Those callers are not lost orders for today. They are people deciding whether you are open on the 26th, whether you do New Year's Eve, whether you cater. A line that answers with accurate information on a day you are closed converts some of them into next week's business at zero marginal cost, and the ones it does not convert at least leave knowing you exist and are operating. The case for covering the closed hours generally is in [24/7 restaurant phone answering](/blog/24-7-restaurant-phone-answering-after-hours).

The version of this that costs you is silence. A number that rings twenty times on a holiday reads, to someone who does not know you, exactly like a restaurant that has closed permanently.

## What to do in the first week of December

Sit down once, for an hour, and write four things: your Christmas Eve open and close, your same-day order cutoff, your per-window pickup cap, and the exact sentence the phone says to someone calling after you close. Push those four into every system that carries hours, and verify each from the customer side.

Then, on December 26, pull the call log for the 24th and count how many calls were answered, how many were hours questions, and how many were orders you turned away. That count is your plan for next year, and it takes ten minutes to produce while the day is still fresh.
