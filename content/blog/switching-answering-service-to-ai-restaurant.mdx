If you already pay a human answering service or an off-site call center, you've solved the "someone always picks up" problem. You're just paying per call or per minute for it, and you've probably noticed the bill spikes on exactly the nights you're busiest.

Moving to an AI voice agent is less a rip-and-replace than a careful handoff. Done right, customers never notice the switch except that the phone stops rolling to voicemail during the rush. Here's what the migration actually looks like, including the parts a vendor won't rush to tell you.

## What actually changes, and what doesn't

Start by separating the two. What *doesn't* change: your phone number, your menu, your signage, your Google listing, your POS. Customers dial the same number they always have.

What *does* change is what happens after the ring. Instead of routing to a call-center agent who takes a message or a basic order for your staff to re-key, the call goes to an AI agent that can take the full order, answer menu questions, and, if you connect it, fire the ticket straight into your POS.

That last part is the real upgrade over most answering services. We wrote a fuller comparison in [AI receptionist vs. a human answering service](/blog/ai-receptionist-vs-answering-service-restaurants). The short version: a message-taking service still leaves someone on your team re-entering orders, and re-entry is where errors and delays creep in.

## Read your current contract before you touch anything

This is the step operators skip and regret. Answering services vary enormously in how easy they are to leave. Some bill month to month with 30 days' notice. Some carry a twelve-month term with a minimum monthly volume, and some charge an early-termination fee that swallows the first quarter of your savings.

Find three things in your agreement: the notice period, the termination terms, and whether your number is registered to them or to you. That third one matters most. If the service ever set up a number on your behalf and you've been publishing it, you're not forwarding a number you control, you're planning a port. Check your Google listing, your menus, and your delivery-app profiles for which number is actually printed on them.

Once you know your notice date, work backward. Your parallel-running window should end shortly after that date, not before it.

## Keep your number: forwarding vs. porting

You have two ways to point calls at the AI, and forwarding is almost always the right first move.

Call forwarding is a setting in your existing phone provider that says "send calls to this other number." It's reversible in minutes, nothing about your account changes, and if you want to bail, you flip it back. Every major business carrier supports it, though the menu path differs, and we've written the steps out carrier by carrier starting with [AT&T](/blog/restaurant-call-forwarding-att).

Porting actually moves the number to a new carrier. It's more permanent, takes days, and there's a brief cutover window where calls can behave oddly. You almost never need it for this, and doing it before you trust the AI is a mistake.

Forward first. Consider porting only much later, if ever, once the AI is clearly your permanent front door.

## Run them in parallel before you commit

The biggest advantage you have over a cold setup is that you don't have to switch everything at once. You already have coverage. Use it.

A sane migration runs both systems side by side for two to four weeks:

- **Route by time window.** Send after-hours and rush-hour overflow to the AI while the human service keeps daytime calls, or vice versa. This lets you compare the AI against your current baseline on real calls.
- **Route overflow only.** Keep the human service as primary and let the AI catch calls that would otherwise hit hold music or voicemail. Every one of those is a call you're currently losing, so it's a low-risk place to start.
- **Read the transcripts daily.** The AI logs every call. Spend ten minutes a day reading them the first week. Most of what looks like an AI mistake is a menu gap you can fix in five minutes.

Don't cut the human service's contract until you've watched the AI handle two real Friday nights. Overlap costs you a little; switching blind costs you more.

## Train the menu before you flip the switch

An answering service works off a script and a message pad. An AI voice agent works off your actual menu, which means the quality of the menu you feed it is the quality of the calls it takes. This is the step that determines whether week one feels smooth or rough.

Have your menu ready as a clean POS export or PDF, not a photo of a laminated sheet with handwritten price changes. Write out the modifiers and combos that currently live in your staff's heads: sizes, sauces, sides, the "you can sub X for Y" rules nobody ever documented. Decide up front which price the phone should quote when dine-in and delivery differ.

Pay particular attention to the names customers say versus the names your POS uses. If callers ask for "the spicy chicken" and your register says "Nashville Hot Chix," that bridge has to be written down, which is the whole subject of [menu naming for voice clarity](/blog/menu-naming-for-voice-clarity). We cover the mechanical side in [how to set up AI phone ordering in about 30 minutes](/blog/how-to-set-up-ai-phone-ordering-in-30-minutes); the setup really is quick, and the menu cleanup is the part that takes real attention.

## Measure the switch honestly

You're moving off a service you're already paying for, so the only question that matters is whether the new setup does better on the things you care about. Pick a few numbers and measure your own rather than trusting anyone's headline stat.

Answer rate during rush is the first. A human service takes one call at a time; an AI takes many at once. Count how many callers used to hit voicemail on a Friday versus now. Order accuracy is the second, and it needs spot-checking tickets against transcripts rather than reading a dashboard. A POS-integrated agent skips the re-keying step, which is where a lot of answering-service errors originate.

Then cost, compared monthly rather than per call. Answering services bill per call or minute and spike at volume. An AI agent is a flat monthly cost, with [Starter at $250/mo for 750 minutes and Professional at $750/mo for 2,000](/pricing), so take your actual minutes from last month's invoice and see which side of the line you land on. Finally, track how often the AI escalated to a person. That number tells you whether you still need human coverage for a slice of calls.

Any real improvement should show up in your own logs within two weeks. If it doesn't, that's data too.

## What a human service still does better

This is the honest part. A human answering service still wins on genuinely high-touch, low-volume, relationship-driven calls: a delicate catering negotiation, an upset regular who wants to be heard, a request so unusual it isn't in any menu or script. A skilled human reads tone and improvises in ways an AI won't match.

If most of your inbound is that kind of call, keep a human in the loop. The right setup lets you escalate those calls to a person while the AI handles the everyday pickup and delivery orders that make up the bulk of the volume, and drawing that line deliberately is part of the setup rather than an afterthought.

Most restaurants find the split lands heavily on the everyday side, which is exactly where the AI's concurrency, flat cost, and clean POS write pay off. But you know your phones better than we do, and the escalation rule is yours to set.

## The test that decides it

Forward your existing number, run both systems for two to four weekends, get your menu clean before you lean on the AI, and measure your own before-and-after instead of taking anyone's word for it.

Then run this test before you cancel anything. On your busiest night, pull every call the AI took and count three things: how many were answered that would previously have rung out, how many produced a correct ticket, and how many needed a person who didn't get one. If the first number is large, the second is near-total, and the third is close to zero, cancel with confidence. If the third number isn't close to zero, you don't have a failed switch, you have an escalation rule to fix.
