"Restaurant voice AI" gets talked about as if it were a single product you either buy or don't. It isn't. It's at least five distinct categories, each built around a different problem, a different restaurant type, and often a different set of vendors. Shop without that map and it's easy to compare a reservations tool against a drive-thru system against an order-taker as though they were interchangeable. They aren't.

Here's the map, and how to tell which shelf you should be standing in front of.

## 1. Drive-thru automation for QSR lanes

This is voice AI at the drive-thru window: taking orders at the lane speaker for high-volume quick-service chains. It's a throughput-and-noise problem, a fixed menu, brutal audio, and pressure not to add seconds to the average car.

The players here include specialists like SoundHound and ConverseNow, plus big-chain in-house builds such as Wendy's FreshAI with Google, and Yum! Brands with Nvidia. It's a QSR-lane category, and it has little to do with a full-service restaurant's phone. We compare the two environments directly in [drive-thru vs. phone ordering](/blog/drive-thru-vs-phone-ordering-voice-ai).

You're on this shelf if you run a QSR with a busy lane and want to automate the window.

## 2. Off-premise phone ordering

This is the category most independent and multi-location restaurants actually mean when they think about voice AI: answering the phone and placing takeout and delivery orders directly into the POS. The hard part isn't the conversation, it's the [order write](/blog/why-pos-integration-depth-matters-more-than-voice-quality), landing a correctly modified ticket the kitchen can make.

Vendors focused here include ConverseNow, Kea, VOICEplug, Bite Buddy, and Loman, among others. X1 Voice sits in this category: 24/7 answering with deep POS sync, direct and through middleware like Deliverect, plus payment taken on the call.

You're on this shelf if you miss phone orders during the rush and want them captured and rung in without staff re-keying them.

## 3. Reservations and answering for full-service

Full-service restaurants have a different phone problem. Fewer orders, more reservations, waitlist questions, hours, "do you have a table for six at eight." The job is answering gracefully and managing the book, not ringing in a modified pizza.

This is the space of tools like Slang.ai and Popmenu's answering product, tuned for the full-service front-of-house voice and the reservation flow. The line between this shelf and the previous one is blurrier than the others, which is why [reservations versus ordering](/blog/voice-ai-reservations-vs-ordering) is worth reading before you assume you need only one.

You're on this shelf if you're a sit-down restaurant drowning in reservation and info calls rather than takeout orders.

## 4. General AI receptionists

Some products are horizontal: an AI receptionist for any small business, a salon, a clinic, a contractor, that happens to also work for a restaurant. They answer, take messages, route calls, and handle FAQs across industries.

Companies like Goodcall and Numa live here, though Numa also does deeper restaurant-specific work, and enterprise conversational platforms like PolyAI power answering at scale across sectors. These tools can be a fine fit for basic coverage. The caution is depth. A general receptionist often isn't built to write a modified food order into your specific POS, which for most restaurants is the entire point.

You're on this shelf if you mainly need calls answered and messages taken, and full order-into-POS capture isn't essential.

## 5. Build-your-own voice platforms

The last category isn't a finished product at all. It's the infrastructure other products are built on. Platforms like Vapi, Retell, and Bland give developers the building blocks, the speech, language, and telephony plumbing, to assemble a custom voice agent.

For a restaurant, this is almost never a buy decision. It's what a vendor or a large group with an engineering team uses to build. We dig into whether that ever makes sense for an operator in our [2026 state of the market](/blog/state-of-voice-ai-for-restaurants-2026) piece.

You're on this shelf if you're a technology team building your own agent, not an operator buying one.

## What buying the wrong shelf feels like

The categories matter because the mistake is quiet. Nothing fails on day one.

Buy a receptionist when you needed an order-taker and the phone gets answered, which feels like a win. Then the messages start arriving as texts and emails, and someone at the host stand re-types them into the POS during the rush. You've moved the labor to a worse moment rather than removed it. The tell is that your order accuracy gets worse even though your answered-call rate got better.

Buy an order-taker when you needed reservations and the reverse happens. The agent handles the trickle of takeout calls beautifully and fumbles the party of eight asking about the patio at eight-thirty, because the book isn't the system it was built around.

Buy a build-your-own platform because the demo was impressive and you discover that the platform is the easy 20 percent. The menu mapping, the modifier trees, the 86 sync, and the POS certification are the rest, and they're an engineering program rather than a configuration weekend.

## How to place yourself in one sentence

The fastest way to find your shelf is to name your actual problem out loud and keep it to one sentence:

- "Automate my drive-thru lane." That's category 1.
- "Stop missing takeout and delivery calls, and get orders into my POS." That's category 2.
- "Manage reservations and info calls at my sit-down spot." That's category 3.
- "Just get the phone answered and messages taken." That's category 4.
- "We're building our own." That's category 5.

Most operators land in category 2 or 3, sometimes both. If you can't pick, your call mix will pick for you: pull a week of calls and count how many were orders versus reservations versus questions. Whichever bucket holds more than half your volume is your category, and the rest is an escalation rule rather than a second purchase.

## The three questions that place a vendor

Once you know your shelf, three questions place any vendor on theirs, and none of them can be answered with a demo reel.

Ask what happens in the seconds after a caller finishes a modified order. Ask which POS systems they hold live, certified connections to, by name, and how many of their customers run that exact one. Ask for the format and size of the reference customer closest to yours, and then call that reference. [What to ask before buying an AI phone system](/blog/what-to-ask-before-buying-an-ai-phone-system) has the longer list.

A vendor who's genuinely in your category answers all three quickly and specifically, because those answers are what they've spent years building. A vendor answering from the next shelf over will be fluent about the conversation and vague about the ticket. That gap is the whole thing you're shopping for.
