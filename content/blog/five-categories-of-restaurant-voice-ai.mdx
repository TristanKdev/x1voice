"Restaurant voice AI" gets talked about as if it were a single product you either buy or don't. It isn't. It's at least five distinct categories, each built around a different problem, a different restaurant type, and often a different set of vendors. Shop without that map and it's easy to compare a reservations tool against a drive-thru system against an order-taker as though they're interchangeable. They aren't.

Here's the map, and how to tell which shelf you should be standing in front of.

## 1. Drive-thru automation (QSR)

This is voice AI at the drive-thru window: taking orders at the lane speaker for high-volume quick-service chains. It's a throughput-and-noise problem — a fixed menu, brutal audio, and pressure to not add seconds to the average car.

The players here include specialists like **SoundHound** and **ConverseNow**, plus big-chain in-house builds such as **Wendy's FreshAI** (with Google) and **Yum! Brands** with **Nvidia**. It's a QSR-lane category, and it has little to do with a full-service restaurant's phone. We compare it to phone ordering in [drive-thru vs. phone ordering](/blog/drive-thru-vs-phone-ordering-voice-ai).

**You need this if:** you run a QSR with a busy drive-thru and want to automate the lane.

## 2. Off-premise phone ordering

This is the category most independent and multi-location restaurants actually mean when they think about voice AI: answering the phone and placing takeout and delivery orders directly into the POS. The hard part isn't the conversation, it's the [order write](/blog/why-pos-integration-depth-matters-more-than-voice-quality) — landing a correctly modified ticket the kitchen can make.

Vendors focused here include **ConverseNow**, **Kea**, **VOICEplug**, **Bite Buddy**, and **Loman**, among others. X1 Voice sits in this category: 24/7 answering with deep POS sync, direct and through middleware like Deliverect, plus payment taken on the call.

**You need this if:** you miss phone orders during the rush and want them captured and rung in without staff re-keying them.

## 3. Reservations and answering (full-service)

Full-service restaurants have a different phone problem. Fewer orders, more reservations, waitlist questions, hours, "do you have a table for six at eight." The job is answering gracefully and managing the book, not ringing in a modified pizza.

This is the space of tools like **Slang.ai** and **Popmenu's** answering product, tuned for the full-service front-of-house voice and the reservation flow.

**You need this if:** you're a sit-down restaurant drowning in reservation and info calls rather than takeout orders.

## 4. General AI receptionists

Some products are horizontal: an AI receptionist for any small business — a salon, a clinic, a contractor — that happens to also work for a restaurant. They answer, take messages, route calls, and handle FAQs across industries.

Companies like **Goodcall** and **Numa** live here (Numa also does deeper restaurant-specific work), and enterprise conversational platforms like **PolyAI** power answering at scale across sectors. These tools can be a fine fit for basic coverage. The caution is depth: a general receptionist often isn't built to write a modified food order into your specific POS, which for most restaurants is the entire point.

**You need this if:** you mainly need calls answered and messages taken, and full order-into-POS capture isn't essential.

## 5. Build-your-own voice platforms

The last category isn't a finished product at all — it's the infrastructure other products are built on. Platforms like **Vapi**, **Retell**, and **Bland** give developers the building blocks (the speech, language, and telephony plumbing) to assemble a custom voice agent.

For a restaurant, this is almost never a buy decision; it's what a vendor or a large group with an engineering team uses to build. We dig into whether that ever makes sense for an operator in our [2026 state of the market](/blog/state-of-voice-ai-for-restaurants-2026) piece.

**You need this if:** you're a technology team building your own agent, not an operator buying one.

## How to place yourself

The fastest way to find your shelf is to name your actual problem in one sentence:

- "Automate my drive-thru lane." → Category 1.
- "Stop missing takeout and delivery calls, and get orders into my POS." → Category 2.
- "Manage reservations and info calls at my sit-down spot." → Category 3.
- "Just get the phone answered and messages taken." → Category 4.
- "We're building our own." → Category 5.

Most operators land in category 2 or 3, sometimes both. Knowing that before you take a single sales call changes the questions you ask and keeps you from being sold the wrong category well.

## The bottom line

Restaurant voice AI is five markets wearing one name. The vendors specialize, the strengths differ, and a tool that's excellent in its home category can be mediocre outside it. Figure out which problem is actually yours, shop that shelf, and treat any "we do all of it" pitch as a prompt to ask where the product really started.
