Restaurant industry stat roundups have a bad habit of citing numbers with no source, or the same unsourced number recycled across a dozen blogs until it looks like established fact. This one tries to do the opposite: where a number comes from a named, findable report, we say so. Where it's an estimate, ours or a reasonable industry approximation, we say that instead.

## Staffing and labor trends

The restaurant industry's staffing challenges are well documented by organizations that track it at scale, rather than any single precise number:

- The **National Restaurant Association's annual State of the Restaurant Industry report** has consistently identified recruiting and retaining staff as one of operators' top-cited operational challenges in recent years, particularly for back-of-house and phone/counter roles during peak shifts.
- **Toast's annual restaurant trends reports** have repeatedly flagged labor cost as a top-three pressure point for independent operators, alongside food cost inflation.
- **Square's restaurant trend reporting** has similarly tracked rising interest in automation and AI-assisted tools among restaurant operators citing labor availability as a driver, which is directionally consistent with what we see in AI phone ordering adoption, though exact adoption percentages vary by report and year.

If you want the underlying numbers rather than our summary of them, search for the current year's edition of each report directly. All three are published annually and the specific figures shift year over year.

## Phone ordering and missed calls: what's estimate vs. sourced

This is the category where you'll see the widest range of numbers across the industry, and where the most caution is warranted.

- **"Restaurants miss roughly 1 in 4 calls during peak hours"** — this is X1 Voice's own estimate, based on aggregated, anonymized analysis of customer call logs as of January 2026. It is not an independently published academic or third-party statistic, and we'd rather say that plainly than borrow false authority from a citation that doesn't exist.
- **Average ticket value on a missed phone order** varies enormously by restaurant type and region. A quick-service missed call and a full-service missed reservation call are not comparable losses. Rather than quote a single blended number, we'd point you to our [missed-call estimation framework](/blog/how-many-calls-does-your-restaurant-miss) to calculate your own.
- **Delivery and phone-order channel mix**: both Toast and Square's trend reports have tracked the shift toward digital ordering channels over the past several years, though phone ordering has remained a meaningful minority channel for many independent restaurants rather than disappearing, especially for regulars who prefer calling in a familiar order.

## Technology adoption trends

- Interest in AI-assisted tools among restaurant operators has grown steadily in recent trend surveys from POS providers, though "interest" and "actual deployed usage" are two different numbers, and reports don't always distinguish them clearly. It's worth reading the methodology section of any report before citing its adoption percentage.
- Quick-service and fast-casual segments have generally led adoption of phone and drive-thru voice AI, largely because of higher call volume relative to staff count during peak windows, compared to full-service restaurants where phone volume is often lower relative to total covers.

## How to use statistics like these responsibly

A few practices we try to hold ourselves to, and that we'd suggest for evaluating any restaurant-tech content (including a competitor's):

- **A number with no named source is an opinion**, not a statistic. Treat it accordingly, regardless of how confidently it's stated.
- **"Our internal data shows"** is a legitimate way to present a company's own estimate, as long as it's labeled as internal rather than dressed up as independent research.
- **Averages hide huge variance** in restaurant data specifically, because a 40-seat full-service restaurant and a 2-window ghost kitchen have almost nothing in common operationally. A single industry-wide average is rarely the right number for your specific restaurant. See our [missed-call framework](/blog/how-many-calls-does-your-restaurant-miss) for a way to get your own.

## The bottom line

If you take one thing from this roundup, take the habit rather than the numbers: ask where a restaurant-tech statistic actually comes from before repeating it, and be wary of any number that's suspiciously precise with no report behind it.
