Fourteen games on the opening Thursday. Tip-offs staggered from around noon to past nine at night, and again on Friday. That is the shape of the first week of the tournament, and it has almost nothing in common with the dinner curve your kitchen is built around.

Orders arrive against tip-offs. Not lunch, not dinner, not a Friday night rush. Twenty to forty minutes before each game starts, a batch of calls comes in from offices, break rooms, dorms, and living rooms, and then it goes quiet until the next one.

## Two weekdays that behave like Saturdays

The opening round is the hard part, and it's hard specifically because it lands on Thursday and Friday afternoons.

You are staffed for a weekday lunch and a light afternoon. Instead you get a lunch surge at 11:30 for the noon games, a second one around 2:00, a third at 4:30, and then a real dinner rush stacked on top of the evening games. Nobody scheduled for that. The prep numbers were built off last Thursday.

### Where the phone breaks

The failure is not usually the kitchen at first. It's the phone during the pre-tip-off window, when eight people call inside ten minutes and one person is answering.

A wing shop with two lines and one host can take maybe three orders in ten minutes if the callers are efficient and none of them have questions. The other five hear a busy signal or ring out, and they call the place down the block. You will never see those in your POS, which is why the day looks merely busy in your reports instead of looking like a day you left money on the counter. The counting method is in [how many calls does your restaurant miss](/blog/how-many-calls-does-your-restaurant-miss), and the same dynamic on a bigger single-day scale is covered in [Super Bowl game day phone orders](/blog/super-bowl-game-day-phone-orders).

### Why the second week is easier

By the Sweet Sixteen the games are on Thursday and Friday evenings and the weekend. Fewer games, more predictable times, and they land closer to when you're already staffed. If you're going to concentrate your planning effort somewhere, put it on the first two days.

The other thing that changes in the later rounds is who's watching. Early rounds pull in bracket-pool crowds who watch whatever is on; later rounds pull in people watching one specific team. If a school near you is still alive, your surge follows that one game and the rest of the schedule stops mattering. Track it, because a regional team in the Elite Eight will produce a bigger night than the entire opening Thursday did.

## Quoting honestly is the whole job

The single most useful discipline during a tournament run is telling people the truth about time.

A caller ordering fifty wings at 6:05 for a 6:10 tip-off already knows they're late. They are not expecting fifteen minutes. What they cannot absorb is being told twenty-five and finding out at the counter that it's an hour, because now they've missed the first half and they blame you rather than their own timing.

Long quotes cost you a small number of orders in the moment. Short quotes that don't hold cost you the rest of the tournament, because someone who waited forty extra minutes on Thursday is not calling you on Saturday. The mechanics of setting a quote you can actually hit are in [quoting accurate pickup times](/blog/quoting-accurate-pickup-times).

The quote also has to move on its own as the queue deepens. If your stated time is a fixed number that a person says out of habit, it will be wrong within twenty minutes of a rush starting. Tying it to actual queue depth is what [order throttling and kitchen capacity](/blog/order-throttling-kitchen-capacity-phone) describes, and it matters more for wings than for almost any other category because your fryers have a hard ceiling.

## The big orders are worth chasing, and they're not takeout

The tournament produces a specific kind of order that most wing shops handle badly: the office or party order in the 100 to 300 wing range, with a named time attached to a game start.

That is a catering order. It has a contact name, a hard deadline, a headcount, and sauce splits that need to be written down accurately or the whole thing is wrong. Treating it as a large takeout ticket that hits the line whenever it surfaces is how you end up firing 200 wings into the middle of a walk-in rush.

Four things make these orders work:

- Get them placed at least a few hours out, and preferably the day before, with a specific pickup minute rather than a vague window.
- Take payment when the order is placed, not at pickup, so a no-show costs the customer instead of you.
- Confirm the sauce breakdown back to the caller by count, not by description, because "half hot, half mild" and "60 hot, 40 mild" produce different tickets.
- Schedule the drop against your prep plan so the order is sequenced rather than queued.

That handling is the same discipline described in [voice AI and large catering orders](/blog/voice-ai-large-catering-orders).

It's also worth naming a cutoff. Past a certain size, a wing order stops being something you can accept an hour before tip-off, because the fryer time alone exceeds the lead time. Pick that number for your kitchen, say it plainly on the phone, and offer the next window instead. A caller told "300 wings needs four hours, I can have them at 8:30 for the second game" books it. A caller told yes and then handed cold wings at 7:40 does not call again.

## Setting up before the bracket comes out

Pull the actual game schedule when it's announced and write your surge windows on the prep sheet: thirty minutes before each tip-off, for every game that starts during your open hours. That gives your kitchen a concrete list instead of a vague sense that this week will be busy.

Set your quoted times per window rather than for the day. The noon window on Thursday and the 9:00 window on Friday are different problems and shouldn't share a number.

Decide who owns the phone during those windows. If the answer is "whoever is free," the answer is nobody, because during a pre-tip-off surge nobody is free. Wing shops running a voice agent get a different answer here, since the pre-game window is exactly the shape of load a phone system absorbs well: many short, similar, order-taking calls arriving at once. The category-specific version of that is in [voice AI for wing shops](/blog/voice-ai-for-wing-shops).

Then, when the tournament ends, compare your answered-call rate during those tip-off windows against a normal afternoon. If it dropped by a third, the tournament didn't just make you busy. It showed you the ceiling on your phone, and that ceiling is there every Friday too, just spread thin enough that you don't notice it.
