Your phone plan is built for people who already know your restaurant. Tourist season fills your line with people who do not, and almost none of what they ask has an answer stored anywhere except in the head of whoever happens to pick up.

Parking. Dress code. How far are you from the pier. Do you take reservations or is it a wait. Is the patio open. Do you have high chairs. Are you the location on Fifth or the other one. A regular has never asked you any of these. A visitor asks three of them in a single ninety-second call.

## The volume is real but the mix is the story

In a seasonal market, summer inbound call volume can run several times the winter baseline. That number gets quoted a lot and it is the less useful half of the picture.

The important change is that a much larger share of calls are informational rather than transactional. They do not end in an order or a reservation. They end in a decision to walk over or not, made by someone holding a phone on a sidewalk two blocks away. You never see the outcome, which is why the loss is easy to miss and easy to underestimate. The method for measuring what you are actually dropping is in [how many calls does your restaurant miss](/blog/how-many-calls-does-your-restaurant-miss).

## What visitors ask that locals do not

The recurring set is short and stable, which is exactly why it is worth handling systematically:

- Where do I park, and is it validated, and is there a lot or only street parking.
- Do you take reservations, do you take walk-ins, and what is the wait right now.
- Are you open, and how late is the kitchen, which is different from how late the bar is.
- Is there a dress code, and can we come in from the beach.
- Is your patio open, do you allow dogs, and do you have space for a stroller.

None of these needs a manager. All of them need a correct, current answer. A host who has been asked about parking forty times that day gives the fortieth caller a shorter answer than the first, and the fortieth caller is the one who decides to go somewhere else. That is the case laid out in [answering FAQs about hours and parking](/blog/voice-ai-answering-faqs-hours-parking).

## The timing is different too

Local call volume tracks meal times. Visitor volume smears across the afternoon and pushes later into the night.

People on vacation eat late, decide late, and call at 9:40 to ask whether the kitchen is still open. In a lot of restaurants nobody answers that call, because at 9:40 the host has been cut and the phone is ringing at an empty stand. Those late calls are the highest-intent ones you get all day, since a person calling at 9:40 wants to eat now. The after-hours pattern is covered in [24/7 restaurant phone answering](/blog/24-7-restaurant-phone-answering-after-hours).

## Accents, languages, and the calls that end in a hang-up

Visitor calls stress speech recognition in ways your regulars never do. Unfamiliar accents, background noise from a street or a boardwalk, a caller reading your name off a map and mispronouncing it, and sometimes a language your staff does not speak.

Do not guess at which languages matter. Listen to a week of calls in season and count. If a real share of your inbound is Spanish or Mandarin or French, that is a concrete number of covers currently ending in a confused hang-up, and it is worth solving. If it is two calls a month, it is not. The accuracy question generally is in [accent and speech recognition accuracy](/blog/voice-ai-accent-speech-recognition-accuracy), and the multi-language handling in [multilingual phone ordering](/blog/multilingual-ai-phone-ordering-restaurants).

The place this bites hardest is address confirmation on a delivery, where a caller unfamiliar with the area reads a hotel name instead of a street. Have a rule for that rather than an improvisation.

## Hotel front desks are a channel, not a nuisance

In a tourist market a nontrivial share of your calls come from concierges and front-desk staff calling on behalf of a guest, or from a guest in a room. These callers behave differently: they are efficient, they book quickly, and they call back if the first attempt was pleasant.

Treat them as a repeat channel. Know which properties send you business, make sure whoever answers can quote your hours and your group capacity without hedging, and do not make a front-desk agent wait on hold, because they will simply call the next restaurant on their list. The adjacent operational picture is in [voice AI for hotel restaurants and room service](/blog/voice-ai-for-hotel-restaurants-room-service).

## What actually changes in your configuration

Seasonal phone setup is mostly answers, not technology. The list is short and it should be written down before the season rather than assembled during it:

- Parking specifics, including the lot name and whether you validate.
- Current walk-in and reservation policy, and what the typical wait is by day part.
- Patio, dog, and stroller answers, which change with weather and get asked constantly.
- Kitchen close time as distinct from bar close time.
- Any summer-only hours, menu, or closed days.

Load these once, keep the wait-time answer current, and the great majority of visitor calls resolve without touching your floor. Restaurant Week and similar local surges produce the same shape of demand, and [restaurant week call volume](/blog/restaurant-week-call-volume) covers that variant.

## Why this season is the wrong time to add phone staff

The instinct is to put a person on the phone for the summer. Look at the economics before you do. You are adding a wage for four months to answer questions that have fixed answers, during hours that do not line up neatly with a shift, and you are training someone in May who leaves in September.

A system that answers every call, gives the same correct parking answer at 9:40 p.m. as at 11 a.m., and hands off the calls that need a person is a better fit for demand that is this lumpy. The comparison in dollars is in [voice AI versus hiring another host](/blog/voice-ai-vs-hiring-another-host).

Here is the check to run this season. Pick one Saturday, log every inbound call, and mark whether it was a question, an order, a reservation, or a complaint. Then mark how many went unanswered. If the question column is the largest and your unanswered count is anything above a handful, you already know what to fix, and you know it from your own line rather than from anyone's pitch.
