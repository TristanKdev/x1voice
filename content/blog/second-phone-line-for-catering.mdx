Most restaurants that ask for a second catering line don't need one. A few genuinely do, and the difference is not volume, it's whether catering is a different business or the same business at a larger size.

Here's the useful version of the question. If a fifty-person order arrives on your main line at 6:40 on a Friday, what happens to it? If the answer is that it gets handled competently by whoever picks up, you don't have a problem a phone number solves. If the answer is that it gets a rushed quote from a host who doesn't know your lead times, or gets a callback promised and never made, you have a routing problem. A second number is one fix for that. It isn't the only one, and it's not usually the cheapest.

## What the second number actually buys

Two things, and it's worth being precise about them because they're often confused.

The first is routing. A separate number can go somewhere else entirely: a manager's phone, an events coordinator, a different queue with different hours. Catering calls stop competing with dinner-rush order traffic, which is the real complaint underneath most requests for a second line. The dynamics of that competition are in [overflow call routing](/blog/overflow-call-routing-busy-line).

The second is measurement. Calls to a distinct number are trivially countable, so you learn how much catering interest you're getting without unpicking it from order volume. That's genuinely useful for deciding whether to hire an events person, and it's a side benefit rather than a reason on its own.

What it does not buy is more catering business. A number is not marketing. If nobody knows the catering line exists, every call still arrives on the main number and you've added an unanswered phone.

## The costs people don't price in

A published phone number is a small permanent obligation.

It goes on your catering page, your PDF menu, your proposals, your invoices, and possibly a separate Google listing. Each of those is a place it can go stale. Restaurants that add a catering line and later change providers routinely leave a dead number on a PDF that's been emailed to four hundred people.

It also has to be answered. A catering line that rings out is worse than no catering line, because the caller specifically chose the number meant for the thing they want to buy and got nothing. If the plan is for it to ring a manager's cell, be honest about what happens when that manager is off, in a walk-in, or driving.

And it needs its own coverage rules. Catering callers plan during business hours on weekdays, which is exactly when your team is doing prep and your phone attention is lowest. A line that only works evenings is aimed at the wrong window.

## When a second number is the right call

A short list. If two or more of these are true, the second number probably earns itself:

- Catering runs on different hours than the restaurant, with a weekday-daytime pattern and a real lead-time policy
- You have a person or a small team whose job includes events, so there's somewhere for the calls to actually land
- Catering has its own pricing structure, deposit rules, and contracts rather than being a big version of your regular menu
- You market catering separately, with its own page, listing, or outbound effort, so the number has somewhere to live
- Corporate accounts call repeatedly and expect to reach the same person, which a general line handles poorly

If you're at one or zero of those, you're describing a restaurant that occasionally sells a large order. Keep one number.

## The version most restaurants should build instead

Route by intent rather than by number.

Whatever answers your main line asks early what the caller needs, then treats a catering inquiry differently from a pickup order: different questions, different information collected, different destination. A fifty-person inquiry gets headcount, date, delivery or pickup, dietary constraints, and a contact, then reaches a person who can quote it. A wing order gets taken and sent to the kitchen.

That's the same routing benefit as a second number, without the second listing, the second greeting, and the second thing to maintain. It also works for the caller who doesn't know the catering number exists, which is most of them. [AI phone answering for catering orders](/blog/ai-phone-answering-catering-orders) covers the mechanics, and [catering lead qualification](/blog/catering-lead-qualification-ai) covers which questions are worth asking before a human gets involved.

One thing to decide deliberately: whether large orders should ever be completed without a person. For most operations the answer is no. A fifty-person order carries a deposit, a lead time, and a delivery window, and the money involved makes a five-minute human conversation cheap insurance. Capture the details, then hand it off. That policy is worked through in [large catering orders](/blog/voice-ai-large-catering-orders).

## If you do add a number, do these four things

Own it. The number should be on an account in your business's name, portable, and transferable if you change providers. A number a vendor holds for you is a number you can't take with you, and that becomes the reason you stay with a provider you've outgrown. [Phone number porting](/blog/phone-number-porting-guide-restaurants) explains what ownership looks like in practice.

Make it local. A local area code reads as a real restaurant to a corporate buyer in a way an 800 number doesn't for a food business. [Local versus toll-free numbers](/blog/local-vs-toll-free-number-restaurants) has the trade-offs.

Publish it in exactly the places catering buyers look, and nowhere else. Catering page, catering menu, proposals, invoices, the events coordinator's signature. Not the main menu. Not the general contact page. Splitting ordinary order calls across two numbers is the failure mode here, and it's easy to cause accidentally.

Give it coverage that matches when catering buyers call, which is weekday daytime, including the hours when your restaurant is closed between lunch and dinner. That gap is when an office manager planning Thursday's lunch will try you.

## The test that settles it

Pull your last three months of catering orders and ask, for each one, how the customer first reached you. Phone, email, the web form, or in person.

If most came by phone, and if you can remember one or two you fumbled or never called back, the routing problem is real and worth fixing this quarter. Whether you fix it with a number or with a better answer on the number you have is a smaller decision than it feels like. If most came by email or the web form, a second phone line will sit quiet, and the work belongs on your catering page instead.
