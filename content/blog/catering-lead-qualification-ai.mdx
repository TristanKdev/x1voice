The most valuable call your restaurant takes this week probably isn't an order. It's someone asking whether you cater a fifty-person office lunch. That call is worth many times a normal ticket, and it's the one most likely to be handled badly — because it comes in during a rush, or after close, and there's no obvious script for it.

The honest framing is that a phone agent shouldn't try to close catering. It should make sure the inquiry gets captured completely and reaches a human fast, with enough detail that the callback is a real conversation. That's a modest-sounding job that turns out to be worth a lot.

## Why catering calls get handled worst

Three structural reasons, all of them about mismatch:

**They arrive at the wrong time.** Catering shoppers call during business hours, which for a restaurant often means lunch service. The person who picks up is mid-rush and not the person who prices events.

**They need a different person.** Whoever owns catering is frequently not near the phone, and might not be in the building. So the call becomes "can I take a message," which is where leads go to die.

**They're not a standard transaction.** A pickup order has a script. A catering inquiry has questions the phone answerer may not be able to answer, which produces a hedging, low-confidence conversation that doesn't inspire booking.

The result is a common pattern: the highest-value inbound call your restaurant receives gets your least prepared response.

## Speed matters more than polish

Here's the reasoning that should shape your whole approach. Someone planning an event is almost never calling one restaurant. They're working a list. Whoever gives them a real answer first has an enormous advantage, not because of any clever sales technique, but because once they've got a workable option they stop calling.

That makes response time the dominant variable. A perfectly polished callback on Monday morning loses to an adequate one on Friday night. It also means the after-hours case matters disproportionately: an inquiry left at 8 p.m. on a voicemail nobody checks until 10 a.m. has had fourteen hours to be solved by a competitor.

This is the same logic behind [24/7 after-hours answering](/blog/24-7-restaurant-phone-answering-after-hours) generally, just with a much bigger number attached to each call.

## The six things to capture

A catering inquiry that reaches your catering person should contain enough for them to prepare before calling back. In practice that means:

1. **Name and callback number.** Obviously, and get the number read back.
2. **Date and time of the event.** Whether you're even available determines everything else.
3. **Headcount.** Fifteen and a hundred and fifty are entirely different conversations.
4. **Service type.** Pickup, drop-off, or staffed. This single field changes the price by a large multiple and it's the one most often missing from a message.
5. **Dietary constraints.** Vegetarian count, allergies, anything religious or medical. Better to surface early than after a menu's been proposed. Our piece on [allergen and dietary questions](/blog/voice-ai-allergen-dietary-questions) covers why the phone is a bad place to make binding safety promises, and that caution applies here too.
6. **Budget or per-head target, if they'll say.** Many won't, and pushing is a mistake. But if it's volunteered, capture it.

That's a two-minute conversation, and it converts a callback from "hi, you called about catering?" into "hi, I've got Thursday the 12th for forty, drop-off — let me walk you through two options."

## What the agent should not do

Be clear about the boundary, both in your configuration and in your expectations:

**Don't quote a firm price.** Catering pricing depends on logistics, staffing, and date, and a number quoted on an inquiry call becomes a number you're arguing about later.

**Don't commit to availability.** "Yes we can do that date" from a system without visibility into your book is a promise you may have to break.

**Don't try to build the menu.** A caller describing a custom spread to a phone agent is a caller having a worse experience than they'd have with a person, and the output won't be usable anyway.

## Routing is the part to actually configure

Capture is only half of it. Where the inquiry goes is what determines whether it gets worked.

Decide, explicitly: does a catering inquiry text a specific person? Email a shared inbox? Both? Does it behave differently at 11 a.m. than at 11 p.m.? A shared inbox that three people half-monitor is functionally the same as voicemail. One named owner with a text notification is dramatically better, even if it's less "systematic."

Also decide what happens if that person doesn't respond. A catering lead with no follow-up rule attached will occasionally fall through, and the cost of that is high enough to justify a simple backstop.

## Measure it, even roughly

You don't need a sophisticated analytics setup. Count catering inquiries per month and count how many you booked. Then track how long it took to call each one back. Even a rough version of that data usually shows a strong relationship between response time and booking, and it makes the case for taking after-hours coverage seriously more concrete than any general argument. It fits into the broader picture of [call metrics worth tracking](/blog/voice-ai-metrics-kpis-restaurants).

## The bottom line

Catering inquiries are the highest-value calls you get and the ones your phone process is least designed for. Don't ask an AI agent to close them. Ask it to answer every one, capture the six fields that make a callback productive, and route it to a named person fast. The competitive advantage in catering is frequently just being the restaurant that responded first with real information — and that's a bar you can clear with process rather than polish.
