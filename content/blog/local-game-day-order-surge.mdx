Every other spike you deal with is a guess. Weather is a guess, a viral post is a guess, a convention downtown is a guess if nobody told you. A home game is on a schedule published months in advance, with a kickoff time attached, and you can put it on a calendar today.

Almost nobody does. The pattern in most bars and pizzerias is that game day arrives, the phone lights up ninety minutes before kickoff, six calls stack up at once, and whoever is closest starts making decisions about which customers exist. Then it happens again in two weeks, identically.

## The curve is tied to kickoff, not to lunch and dinner

Your normal day has meal periods. Game day does not care about them.

Orders start climbing around three hours before kickoff and peak somewhere near the ninety-minute mark, as people finalize what they are feeding whoever is coming over. That first wave is heavy on volume orders: wings by the fifty, party trays, six pizzas going to one address. It is also heavy on delivery, and the delivery window matters more than usual, because arriving after kickoff is a failure regardless of how good the food is.

Then it goes quiet. Genuinely quiet, for most of the game, which is where the mistake happens. Managers read the lull as the rush being over and send someone home or move the phone back to the host stand.

The second wave hits within a few minutes of the end. Post-game orders skew smaller, faster, and more emotional, and on a win they are much larger than on a loss. That is not a statistic anyone should quote at you, it is something every bar manager near a stadium will tell you and something you can verify against your own numbers in one afternoon.

### Which games matter to you

Not all of them will. Run the comparison before you build a plan around it.

Take last season's home dates, pull sales and call volume for each, and compare against the same weekday in a week with no home game. Look at rivalry games and nationally televised games separately, because those behave differently from a Tuesday conference matchup. Also check whether the effect reverses for you, since a restaurant in an office district can lose business on a big game while a delivery-heavy operation across town gains it. If you have never looked at call volume this way, [call volume forecasting for restaurants](/blog/call-volume-forecasting-restaurants) covers pulling the numbers.

Do this once and you have a working calendar for the next season, plus a defensible reason to schedule differently on eight specific dates.

## Pre-orders are the only thing that actually flattens the peak

You cannot cook a two-hour surge in two hours. The kitchen's capacity is what it is, and the honest options are to turn away orders, quote long waits, or move production earlier.

Timed pre-orders move production earlier. A customer ordering Thursday for a Sunday one o'clock pickup lets you prep on Sunday morning instead of during the crush. The mechanics are the same as any timed pickup system: fifteen-minute slots, a hard count of orders per slot, and a phone that stops offering a slot once it fills. The counting discipline is identical to bulk holiday work, and the version written out for barbecue in [Labor Day pickup windows](/blog/labor-day-bbq-orders) transfers directly.

The part that breaks is the cap. Someone calls, their slot is full, they push, and a manager makes an exception. Four exceptions later the slot has nine orders in it and the counter is a mob. If you are going to use slots, the cap has to be enforced by whatever answers the phone rather than negotiated per call, which is one of the few genuine advantages of taking a person out of that particular decision.

Publish the pre-order deadline too. Orders close at a stated hour the day before, and after that you are taking same-day only, subject to the wait. Callers respect a deadline they were told about and resent one they discover.

## The phone is where the day is won or lost

Here is what a busy game day looks like from the customer's side. They call, it rings eight times, nobody picks up. They call the next place. You never find out this happened, which is why the loss stays invisible year after year and why operators consistently underestimate it. The arithmetic is laid out in [the real cost of a missed restaurant phone call](/blog/the-real-cost-of-a-missed-restaurant-phone-call), and it is worse on a game day than on a normal Friday, because the ticket sizes are bigger.

Three things make the phone work during the surge, in order of how much they matter.

The first is that someone or something answers every call. Not most calls. Six simultaneous rings is not a problem a host can solve by trying harder. Either you have a person whose only job is the phone during those two hours, sitting somewhere other than the door, or you move the calls off the floor entirely. This is the narrow, honest case for automating a phone: a hard, short, predictable capacity ceiling that no amount of hustle clears. It is also why [game day phone volume](/blog/super-bowl-game-day-phone-orders) reads differently from ordinary peak-hour advice.

The second is that the wait time you quote is true. A caller told forty-five minutes will usually still order. A caller told twenty who waits fifty will not order again. Whoever answers needs a current number from the kitchen, updated during the rush, and permission to say no once you are past a cutoff. If software is answering, that quote has to be something you can change from a phone in your pocket at five past noon.

The third is that the menu the phone knows matches what the kitchen can still make. Selling wings after wings run out on a game day is the single most reliable way to turn a good day into a bad one, because the customer finds out at pickup, twenty minutes before kickoff, with no time to fix it anywhere else. Availability has to flow from the POS rather than from a printed sheet somebody meant to update, which is the argument in [why POS integration depth matters](/blog/why-pos-integration-depth-matters-more-than-voice-quality).

## What to set up this week, before the season

Put the home dates in a shared calendar with kickoff times. Mark the two waves on each one. Then decide, per date, three things: your pre-order cutoff, your slot capacity, and who owns the phone from two hours before kickoff until an hour after.

If the answer to that last one is "whoever is free," you have already decided to miss calls on your eight best days of the year. That is a legitimate decision if the volume genuinely does not justify anything else. Make it on purpose, with last season's numbers in front of you, rather than by default at ninety minutes to kickoff.
