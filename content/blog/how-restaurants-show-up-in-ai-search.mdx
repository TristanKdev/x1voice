You show up in AI search by being present, accurate, and consistent across the sources these systems draw on. That's the whole answer, and the reason it's not more satisfying is that there's no submission form, no keyword to target, and no confirmed ranking mechanism to game. Systems that answer questions about restaurants are synthesizing from what's verifiably known about you, so the work is making sure what's knowable is correct.

This piece is the practical version: what these systems appear to draw on, what you can actually influence, and what nobody honestly knows.

## What "AI search" means here

Several different things get lumped together, and they behave differently:

- **AI assistants** answering a question conversationally, sometimes with live web access.
- **Search engines summarizing** above the traditional link results.
- **Map and local search** surfaces that have used structured business data for years and are increasingly generating summaries from it.
- **Voice assistants** on phones and speakers answering "who delivers pizza near me."

They differ in how they source information, and none publish their weighting. What they share is a reliance on structured business data and on being able to verify a fact from more than one place. That commonality is what makes a general strategy possible even without knowing the specifics.

## The five sources that seem to matter

Based on what these systems clearly reference, roughly in order:

**1. Your Google Business Profile.** For anything local, this remains the backbone. Hours, address, phone, category, attributes. It's also the easiest to fix and the most often neglected — profiles with hours from two years ago are extremely common.

**2. Your own website.** The authoritative source for your menu, your story, and your details, provided it's readable. A menu that only exists as a PDF or a photo is close to invisible.

**3. Delivery and ordering platforms.** Whether you love the commissions or not, these carry menu data and are frequently referenced. Which is another reason to keep them accurate even while you're trying to [move volume to direct channels](/blog/voice-ai-vs-delivery-app-commissions).

**4. Review platforms.** Reviews inform the qualitative parts of an answer — what you're known for, whether you're good for groups, whether service is fast.

**5. Local mentions.** Press, neighborhood blogs, roundups. Least controllable, and it's the layer where the honest advice is just to be a restaurant people write about.

## What you can actually control

Sorted by effort against likely payoff.

### Fix contradictions first

If your hours say 10 p.m. on your website, 9 p.m. on Google, and 11 p.m. on a delivery app, a system answering "how late are they open" has to choose. It may choose wrong, and you'll never know it happened.

Spend an hour finding every place your basic facts appear and making them agree. This is the highest-return task on the list and it requires no expertise. The [AEO fundamentals piece](/blog/what-is-answer-engine-optimization-restaurants) covers why consistency does more work than any single optimization.

### Make your menu machine-readable

Text on a page, with prices, in a normal HTML structure. Not a PDF. Not an image. Not a graphic embedded in a design tool's export. If you want "do they have gluten-free options" answered correctly, that information has to be readable as text.

### Answer real questions on your site

The questions your phone gets are the questions people ask assistants: parking, delivery area, dietary options, whether you take reservations, what your busiest times are. A page that answers them plainly gives these systems something to draw on. It also, usefully, gives you the exact content you need to configure your [phone agent's FAQ answers](/blog/voice-ai-answering-faqs-hours-parking) — same facts, two channels.

### Add structured data

Restaurant schema markup states your facts unambiguously rather than leaving them to be inferred from layout. It's a modest technical task and it removes guesswork.

### Keep review profiles claimed and current

Not because you can control reviews, but because an unclaimed profile with stale information is a source of contradictions.

## What nobody actually knows

Being honest about the limits, since this is a field with a lot of confident advice and not much verifiable evidence:

- How each system weights sources. Not published, and it changes.
- Whether being mentioned in AI answers drives meaningful volume for a given restaurant, or how that compares to existing channels.
- Whether tactics beyond the fundamentals produce any measurable difference.
- How the surfaces themselves will look in a year.

The practical implication is to invest in the durable layer — accurate, consistent, structured facts about your business — and be skeptical of anything more elaborate. The fundamentals help you regardless of which system someone uses, including the ones that don't exist yet. Anyone offering guaranteed AI search placement is selling certainty that isn't available.

## Checking on yourself

Not rigorous, but useful: periodically ask a few assistants the questions your customers would ask. "What time does [your restaurant] close?" "Does [your restaurant] deliver?" "What's good at [your restaurant]?"

You'll usually find something wrong — old hours, a stale phone number, a menu item you dropped last year. That's the point. It's a cheap diagnostic that tells you where the contradictions are, and fixing what it surfaces is exactly the work that matters.

## The part everyone forgets

Here's the thing worth ending on. All of this is about getting found, and getting found produces a phone call.

A restaurant that does the AEO work well, gets surfaced in an answer, and then doesn't answer the phone during the rush has converted effort into a missed opportunity. The whole point of being discoverable is the call, and the [call that rings out](/blog/how-many-calls-does-your-restaurant-miss) is where the funnel actually leaks for most operators.

So treat phone coverage as part of the same project rather than a separate one. Being easy to find and being unreachable is a strange combination to invest in, and it's more common than it should be.

## The bottom line

Restaurants show up in AI answers by being consistently and verifiably documented across the sources these systems read. Fix your contradictions, get your menu on the page as text, answer common questions plainly, add structured data, and keep your listings claimed. Check yourself by asking assistants about your own restaurant occasionally. And make sure that when the work pays off and the phone rings, somebody — or something — picks up.
