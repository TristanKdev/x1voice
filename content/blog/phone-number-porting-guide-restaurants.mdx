Your restaurant's phone number is one of the few pieces of infrastructure that's genuinely hard to change. It's printed on packaging and menus, listed on Google and every delivery platform, painted on a window somewhere, and saved in the contacts of every regular you have. Porting it to a new provider is routine, and it's also the kind of routine that occasionally goes wrong in ways that cost you a Friday night.

The two things that matter most: get the account details exactly right on the request, and consider whether you need to port at all — forwarding is often lower risk and reversible.

## What porting actually is

Porting moves your existing phone number from one carrier or provider to another while keeping the number itself. The number stops being served by your old provider and starts being served by the new one.

The important consequence is that it's a real cutover. At some point, calls stop going one place and start going another. That transition is where the risk lives.

Forwarding is the alternative and it works differently. Your number stays where it is, and your current provider sends incoming calls to another destination. Nothing about your carrier relationship changes, and you can undo it in minutes.

## Consider forwarding before porting

This deserves its own section because it's the option operators most often skip past.

If you're adding a new phone system — including an automated answering agent — forwarding your existing number to it accomplishes the same thing operationally with far less risk. Calls to your published number arrive at the new system, your old number and account stay intact, and if it doesn't work out you turn forwarding off.

Reasons you might port anyway: consolidating providers to reduce cost, your current provider's service is genuinely bad, or you need features only available on the new platform. Those are legitimate. But if the only goal is "calls should go to the new system," ask whether forwarding gets you there. Any vendor should be able to answer this clearly — it's on the list in [what to ask before buying an AI phone system](/blog/what-to-ask-before-buying-an-ai-phone-system).

## Before you start: gather the exact details

Most port rejections come from information mismatches. The new provider submits a request, the old carrier compares it to their records, and anything that doesn't match causes a rejection and a restart.

Pull a recent bill from your current provider and copy, literally:

- The account number as it appears on the bill
- The billing telephone number, which may not be the number you're porting
- The exact business name on the account, including punctuation and any "LLC" or "Inc"
- The service address exactly as the carrier has it, not as you'd write it
- Any account PIN or passcode the carrier requires for porting

The frequent trap is that these details reflect however the account was set up years ago, possibly by a previous owner or a manager who no longer works there. What's on the bill wins over what you remember.

## The sequence that avoids downtime

1. **Confirm what you have.** Inventory every number in use — the main line, any secondary lines, the fax line nobody uses, delivery-specific numbers. Decide which are actually porting.
2. **Set up the new system fully first.** It should be configured, tested, and ready to take calls before the number arrives. Porting into an unconfigured system is how you get a Friday night with no phone.
3. **Submit the port request** with the exact details above and let the new provider drive it.
4. **Do not cancel your old service.** Cancelling before a port completes can cause the number to be released, and recovering it ranges from difficult to impossible. Let the port itself close the old service.
5. **Schedule the cutover deliberately.** Ask for a window during your slowest hours — a Monday or Tuesday morning rather than a Friday afternoon.
6. **Test immediately after cutover.** Call from an outside line, a mobile on a different carrier, and if possible a landline. Verify calls ring, audio works both directions, and voicemail behaves.
7. **Keep the old provider reachable** for a short period in case something needs unwinding.

## What to check after the port

The port completing isn't the same as everything working. A few things commonly break quietly:

- **Caller ID name.** Your registered business name may not carry over automatically. Verify what customers see and re-register if needed — see [restaurant caller ID and branded calling](/blog/restaurant-caller-id-branded-calling).
- **Text messaging.** If customers text your number, or you send order updates, confirm messaging is enabled on the new provider. This is a frequent gap.
- **Spam labeling.** Check whether the number's reputation followed it. If new complaints appear, see [fixing a "Spam Likely" label](/blog/fixing-spam-likely-caller-id-restaurant).
- **Voicemail.** Old voicemail generally does not transfer. Record a new greeting immediately, since the default one is worse than nothing.
- **E911 and address registration.** Make sure the new provider has your correct service address on file.
- **Integrations.** Anything pointed at your phone system — POS, delivery platforms, a reservation tool — may need reconfiguring.

## Where your number appears (and what to update)

Even with a successful port, spend an hour updating everywhere the number is referenced, especially if any of it is wrong or points at a secondary line:

Google Business Profile, your website, delivery marketplace listings, third-party directories, social profiles, printed menus and packaging, signage, and any advertising. The listing entries matter most because they drive the most traffic.

## Multi-location complications

Groups porting several locations at once should stage it rather than doing them simultaneously. Port one location, verify everything for a week, then proceed. A configuration mistake caught at one location is a nuisance; the same mistake replicated across twelve is a bad month.

Also watch for shared account structures where multiple locations sit under one billing number, which complicates partial ports. Ask your current carrier about this before submitting anything. Broader multi-location considerations are in [voice AI for multi-location franchise groups](/blog/voice-ai-multi-location-franchise-groups).

## The bottom line

Porting is routine but not risk-free, and your number is one of the hardest things in your business to replace. Before you port, seriously consider whether forwarding gets you the same outcome with a fraction of the risk and full reversibility. If you do port, pull a current bill and copy the account details exactly, get the new system fully configured before the number arrives, never cancel the old service yourself, and schedule the cutover for your slowest hours. Afterward, verify caller ID, texting, voicemail, and integrations rather than assuming they came along. And if you run multiple locations, do them one at a time.
